Paul Crash Course

David Jivan · davidjivan.net

Here's the wild thing: Paul founded Christianity more than Jesus did.

Jesus of Nazareth was a Jewish apocalyptic preacher who healed people, argued with scribes, got executed by the Romans, and whose followers claimed he was raised from the dead. Within his lifetime he had no "church" to speak of — a following, yes. A movement, barely. After his death, his closest disciples (Peter, James, John) stayed in Jerusalem and kept observing Torah. They didn't seem to think they'd started a new religion.

Then Paul happened.

A Diaspora Jew from Tarsus who never met Jesus in the flesh. A Pharisee who had been actively persecuting the Jesus movement. He had a visionary experience he understood as an encounter with the risen Christ. Within twenty years, he had planted communities across the eastern Mediterranean, written the earliest documents that would become the New Testament, and formulated a theology that turned a small Jewish apocalyptic sect into something that would eventually conquer the Roman Empire.

Paul's letters are the earliest Christian texts we have. They predate the Gospels by roughly 20 years. They are our closest access point to the first decades of the Jesus movement. And they are the work of a man who is, depending on who you ask, the greatest theologian in Christian history, a traumatized visionary projecting his own psychology onto the cosmos, or someone who accidentally described a consciousness-first metaphysics fifteen centuries before anyone had the vocabulary for it.

This crash course is about the Paul we can actually know — the man behind the letters, the letters he actually wrote, the ones someone else wrote in his name, and what to make of all of it.

The authentic Paul: what we know

Most of what the average person "knows" about Paul comes from the Acts of the Apostles — probably written a generation or two after Paul's death, though dates range widely (anywhere from the early 60s to the 90s CE). The identity of the author is genuinely debated: the "we" passages (where the narrative shifts to first-person plural) could indicate a genuine travel companion, or they could be a literary convention common in ancient sea-voyage narratives — scholars disagree. Either way, Acts is a theological narrative, not neutral biography. It smooths Paul's edges, harmonizes his conflicts with the Jerusalem apostles, and presents a version of Paul that serves its narrative purposes.

The real Paul has to be reconstructed from his own letters. Here's what we can say with reasonable confidence:

  • He was a Diaspora Jew from Tarsus (in modern-day Turkey). A Hellenistic Jewish city, not Jerusalem. He was fluent in Greek, trained in Greek rhetorical tradition, and knew the Septuagint (the Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures) intimately.
  • He was a Pharisee. This meant rigorous Torah observance and a belief in resurrection — which set Pharisees apart from the Sadducees. His Pharisaic training shaped how he read scripture: typologically, argumentatively, finding patterns and prefigurements everywhere.
  • He was a Roman citizen. This is one of the few things from Acts that the letters don't contradict. Citizenship gave him legal protections and probably shaped how he navigated the empire.
  • He never met Jesus. Not once. His apostolic authority was entirely based on his visionary encounter with the risen Christ (Gal 1:11-12, 1 Cor 15:8). This was a point of tension with the Jerusalem apostles who had known Jesus in the flesh — and Paul was defensive about it.
  • He actively persecuted the Jesus movement before his vision. We don't know exactly what this meant — arrest, imprisonment, beatings, possibly complicity in executions. He mentions it himself (Gal 1:13, 1 Cor 15:9, Phil 3:6), and the guilt or moral weight of it surfaces in ways that matter for reading him.
  • His "conversion" was not a conversion. Paul never stopped being a Jew. He understood his encounter with Christ as a prophetic call to a specific mission: bringing the message of Israel's Messiah to the nations. The parallel he draws is to Jeremiah and Isaiah (Gal 1:15) — called from the womb, sent to the Gentiles. He never uses the language of "conversion." He uses the language of prophetic commissioning.
  • He thought the end was coming any day. Paul believed he was living in the last generation (1 Thess 4:17, 1 Cor 7:29-31). The resurrection of Jesus was the first stage of the general resurrection of the dead, and the full arrival of God's kingdom was imminent. This shaped everything — his ethics (don't marry if you can help it, the time is short), his community organization (no elaborate institutional structures needed), his sense of urgency.
  • He was in real conflict with the Jerusalem apostles. Galatians 2 describes a confrontation with Peter in Antioch over table fellowship with Gentiles. Paul frames it as a fight about the truth of the gospel. This was not a disagreement among friends — Paul says he opposed Peter "to his face." The Jerusalem council (Acts 15) smooths this over; Paul's own account (Gal 2) shows it was uglier than Acts lets on.
  • He lost the argument — and then history proved him right. The Jerusalem church (Jewish Christians who kept Torah) faded from history. Paul's Gentile-inclusive, non-Torah-observant version of the Jesus movement became Christianity. The Ebionites and other Jewish Christian groups persisted for a few centuries and then disappeared. The religion that conquered Rome was Paul's religion, not Peter's.
  • He died under Nero, probably in the mid-60s CE. The details are uncertain. Tradition says he was beheaded (Roman citizenship spared him crucifixion). His letters stop around 60 CE, and Acts ends with him under house arrest in Rome. What happened after that is conjecture, but his absence from later church memory is consistent with execution in the Neronian persecution.

The seven undisputed letters

Modern scholarship is nearly unanimous on seven letters that Paul certainly wrote. The consensus is as strong as anything gets in biblical studies:

Romans~57 CEPaul's most sustained theological argument. Addressed to a community he hadn't founded and hadn't visited. The letter that launched a thousand Reformation debates — most of which are about what Luther read into it, not what Paul was actually saying.
1 Corinthians~54 CEA community in crisis. Factions, sexual ethics, lawsuits, food offered to idols, resurrection disagreements, women prophesying, chaos at the Lord's supper. Paul as firefighter, not theologian.
2 Corinthians~55-57 CEThe most personal and raw. Paul defending his apostolic authority, describing his sufferings, his "thorn in the flesh," his vision of being caught up to the third heaven. The closest we get to Paul's inner life.
Galatians~50-55 CEThe angry letter. Paul furious that the Galatians are being persuaded to adopt Torah observance. No thank you, no warm opening — he gets straight to "I can't believe you're this stupid." The letter that contains the line about opposing Peter to his face.
Philippians~55-60 CEThe warm letter. A community Paul loved and who supported him. The Christ-hymn (2:6-11) — possibly a pre-Pauline hymn he quotes — is the densest early Christological statement in the New Testament.
1 Thessalonians~50 CEThe earliest Christian document we have. A short, pastoral letter to a young community. Almost all eschatology — Paul expecting Christ's return within their lifetimes.
Philemon~55 CEA personal letter about a runaway slave named Onesimus. The only undisputed letter that doesn't address a community. Short enough to read in five minutes. Reveals a different Paul — personal, diplomatic, cagey.

Why these seven and not the others? Vocabulary analysis, theology, historical references, manuscript evidence, and stylistic consistency all converge. The undisputed letters share a characteristic vocabulary (specific particles, connective phrases, grammatical tics), a consistent theological profile (eschatological urgency, participatory Christology, Spirit-language), and a historical situation that matches mid-1st-century conditions. The disputed letters diverge on all three — measurably so. The scholarship is about as settled as it gets for ancient texts. When a pastor tells you "scholars debate whether Paul wrote Ephesians," what they're not saying is: the debate is effectively over and the answer is no.

The deutero-Pauline letters

These are the letters attributed to Paul that almost certainly were not written by him. Scholarship divides them into two groups:

01
The disputed three
Ephesians · Colossians · 2 Thessalonians
[+]
The case against

Ephesians: Nearly 70% of the vocabulary in Ephesians doesn't appear in the undisputed letters. The sentence structure is different (longer, more liturgical). The theology shifts from "in Christ" as participation to "in Christ" as a spatial location — "seated with him in the heavenly places." The eschatological urgency is gone; this is a settled, cosmic Christology, not an apocalyptic one. Most scholars place it in the 90s CE — a generation after Paul, written by an admirer who saw himself as Paul's successor.

Colossians: Closer to Paul than Ephesians but still off. The Christological language is more developed ("the firstborn of all creation," "in him all things hold together"). The household code (Col 3:18-4:1) is more formal than anything in the undisputed letters. The vocabulary and style diverge from the seven. Some scholars defend it as genuinely Pauline; the majority view is that it's deutero-Pauline, written by a close associate.

2 Thessalonians: Oddly, the main evidence against it is how similar it is to 1 Thessalonians — too similar. It reads like someone copying Paul's style, repeating his themes, but flattening the eschatology. The "man of lawlessness" passage (2:3-12) doesn't sound like Paul. The signature at the end (3:17) — "this is my own handwriting" — may be the forger's attempt to authenticate the forgery.

02
The Pastorals
1 Timothy · 2 Timothy · Titus
[+]
The case against

The consensus here is stronger than for the disputed three. The Pastorals are almost universally regarded as pseudonymous, likely from the early 2nd century. The differences are stark:

The vocabulary is entirely different — over 300 words appear in the Pastorals that never appear in the undisputed letters. The church structure is institutional: bishops, deacons, elders, formal qualifications for office. The eschatological urgency is completely absent — Paul's "the time is short" has become "guard the deposit of faith." The theological concerns are about orthodoxy, sound teaching, and orderly worship — not about the Spirit, not about participation in Christ's death and resurrection.

And then there are the household codes. The undisputed Paul wrote "there is no longer Jew or Greek, slave or free, male and female; for all of you are one in Christ Jesus" (Gal 3:28). The Pastorals write "women should be silent in the churches" (1 Tim 2:11-12), "let them learn with full submission." This is not Paul developing his thought. This is a later generation using Paul's authority to solve problems Paul never had.

What changed, and why

Reading the undisputed Paul alongside the deutero-Pauline letters is like watching a photograph develop in reverse. The image gets clearer, sharper, more institutional — and less recognizably Paul.

The differences cluster around three axes:

Eschatology. The authentic Paul thought the end was imminent. His ethics were shaped by that conviction: singleness is better than marriage because the time is short (1 Cor 7); don't get tangled in worldly concerns; the Lord is at hand (Phil 4:5). The Pastorals have no such urgency. They assume an indefinite future — plan for it, build institutions, appoint leaders who will outlast you. The difference is not nuance. It's a different worldview.

Ecclesiology. The authentic Paul's communities were charismatic — led by apostles, prophets, teachers, people with spiritual gifts. There are no bishops in the undisputed letters. The Pastorals have a formal hierarchy: bishops, deacons, elders, with published qualifications. The authentic Paul wrote about the body of Christ where every member has a gift and a function. The Pastorals write about order, sound doctrine, and who gets to be in charge.

Social ethics. The authentic Paul's "no longer Jew or Greek, slave or free, male and female" (Gal 3:28) is a genuine leveling statement — even if Paul didn't fully live it out in practice (he told slaves to stay as they were in 1 Cor 7:21), the theological principle was radical. The household codes in the Pastorals are the opposite: they reinforce social hierarchy — wives submit, slaves obey, women be silent. Reading them side by side with the undisputed Paul, it's hard to avoid the conclusion that someone was walking Paul's theology backward.

Why would someone do this? Pseudonymity was a recognized practice in the ancient world, especially within philosophical schools. A student writing in the master's name was not necessarily considered fraud — it was a way of honoring the tradition and extending it to new situations. The Pastoral Epistles were almost certainly written by someone who saw themselves as Paul's legitimate heir, addressing problems Paul never faced: the delay of the parousia, the rise of competing teachers, the need for institutional stability. The motive wasn't deception in the modern sense. It was: "Paul would say this if he were alive, so I'll say it for him." Whether that's acceptable is up to you. But calling it "forgery" with modern connotations of criminal fraud is anachronistic.

Three perspectives on Paul's psyche

Who was Paul, really? A man who never met Jesus, who persecuted the Jesus movement, who had a vision that changed everything, who traveled constantly, who was beaten, imprisoned, shipwrecked, who argued with everyone, who refused to back down on Gentile inclusion, who saw himself as the apostle to the nations, who wrote some of the most electrically alive religious texts in human history — and who died alone in a Roman prison cell, probably executed by the state he had claimed citizenship of.

Here are three ways to understand him.

1. The apocalyptic Jewish mystic

This is the reading that has dominated serious scholarship for the last fifty years, following E.P. Sanders' Paul and Palestinian Judaism (1977). On this reading, Paul is not a convert from Judaism to Christianity. He is an apocalyptic Jew who believed that the age to come had broken into the present age through the resurrection of the Messiah. He did not reject Judaism. He reinterpreted it in light of what he believed was the turning of the ages.

The key move: Paul's "righteousness by faith" is not about how an individual gets saved — that's Luther reading his own anxieties back into Paul. It's about how Gentiles become members of the people of God. The question Paul was actually answering was: "How do non-Jews enter the covenant community?" His answer: through faith in the Messiah, not through Torah observance. The "works of the law" Paul opposes are not "trying to earn your salvation by good deeds" — they're the boundary markers of Jewish identity (circumcision, food laws, calendar observance) that kept Gentiles out. Paul's argument was: the Messiah has come, the turning of the ages has begun, and the boundary markers have been superseded. Gentiles come in as Gentiles.

On this reading, Paul is a mystic who experienced the risen Christ as a reality so overwhelming that it restructured everything he thought he knew about God, scripture, and the people of Israel. His "in Christ" language is participationist — not a metaphor but an ontological claim: believers are literally incorporated into the Messiah's body, share his death and resurrection, and are being transformed into his likeness. This is not forensic (declared righteous). It is mystical and embodied.

The texts that anchor this reading: the Philippians Christ-hymn (2:6-11), the Adam/Christ typology in Romans 5, the "in Christ" formula that saturates all seven letters, the language of being "crucified with Christ" (Gal 2:19-20), the baptismal union with Christ's death (Rom 6:3-11).

Key scholars: E.P. Sanders, James D.G. Dunn, N.T. Wright, Krister Stendahl. The "New Perspective on Paul" broadly follows this line, though they disagree with each other on the details.

2. The traumatized visionary

This reading starts from a different question: what kind of man persecutes a religious movement and then has a vision that makes him its most passionate advocate?

Paul tells us he was "violently persecuting" the church (Gal 1:13) and that he was "a blasphemer, a persecutor, a man of violence" (1 Tim 1:13 — even if the Pastorals aren't Paul's, the tradition remembered him this way). We don't know exactly what this meant, but the word Paul uses (ekportheō in Gal 1:23 — "was destroying it") is strong. He was not a passive bystander. He was active, aggressive, and effective.

Then he saw the risen Christ. The Damascus road experience, on this reading, was not a calm theological realization. It was a shattering. A persecutor who was certain he was serving God was suddenly confronted with the possibility that he was fighting against God. The vision didn't just change his theology. It broke something open in him that had been tightly closed.

The evidence for this reading is in the letters — not in what Paul says directly, but in what leaks through. The defensiveness about his apostolic authority. The way he pivots from boasting about his credentials to calling them "rubbish" (Phil 3:8). The "thorn in the flesh" (2 Cor 12:7) — a "messenger of Satan" that torments him, which he begged God to remove and was told "my grace is sufficient." What was it? The traditional guesses: an eye condition, a speech impediment, migraines, epilepsy. A psychological reading might ask: was it the persecutor's guilt, surfacing as a physical or spiritual affliction that never fully healed?

On this reading, Paul's relentless activity — the travel, the planting, the arguing, the suffering — is not just apostolic zeal. It's a drivenness. He is trying to outrun or atone for the person he was before the vision. The man who destroyed communities of believers is now building them, at enormous cost to himself. The man who was certain he was right has been broken open into a different kind of certainty — not the calm confidence of the Pharisee but the restless urgency of someone who knows how wrong he could have been.

This reading doesn't reduce Paul to pathology. It takes seriously the violence of his transformation and asks what it cost him — and what it made possible.

Key scholars: Paula Fredriksen, John G. Gager, Alan F. Segal (the latter's Paul the Convert reads Paul's experience through the lens of Jewish mystical traditions and conversion psychology).

3. The accidental metaphysician

This is the reading I bring to Paul — and I want to be explicit that it's my reading, not something the texts force.

Paul was not a philosopher. He was an apocalyptic Jew with a prophetic vocation. He had no interest in metaphysics as a discipline. But the structure of his thought — the shape of his claims about Christ, the soul, and the cosmos — maps onto a consciousness-first metaphysics in ways that are genuinely surprising.

Consider the key Pauline claim: "It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me; yet the life I now live in the flesh, I live" (Gal 2:20).

On a traditional reading, this is devotional language — Paul expressing his personal piety. On the consciousness-first reading, it's a structural claim. The "I" is the soul — the bounded, localized perspective within universal consciousness. "Christ" is the Logos — the rational structure of the Ground as it expresses itself in and through the soul. The first clause ("no longer I, but Christ") describes the amnesia dissolving: the soul recognizing that its own deepest life was never its own private possession. The second clause ("yet I live") is the anti-monism railing: the soul does not dissolve into the Ground. It returns, transformed, still genuinely a localized perspective. "The soul that doesn't forget is still a soul" — Paul stated it before anyone had the vocabulary for it.

Paul's "in Christ" language, which appears over 150 times in the undisputed letters, is the same structure in different words. "In Christ" is not a spatial location or a metaphor for belonging to a group. It's Paul's way of saying that the soul's true environment — the medium in which it lives, moves, and has its being — is the Logos. The Ground is not distant. The soul is in it, and it is in the soul. Paul was a first-century Jew working with apocalyptic categories. He did not have a three-level ontology. But the architecture he was describing — a bounded self that participates in a larger reality that is both its origin and its destination — is the same architecture that the consciousness-first frame describes.

The convergence is not proof of anything. Paul was not an idealist philosopher. The consciousness-first reading is an overlay I bring to the text, and I own that. But the convergence is worth noticing: a first-century Jewish mystic, describing his experience of transformation, reaches for the same structural shape that a non-dual idealist metaphysics describes. When two traditions, working from completely different starting points, point at the same structure, the convergence is signal.

The "yet I live" clause — the thing that has always been hardest for interpreters to explain — is the gift Paul gives the consciousness-first reading. He knew, from inside his own experience, that the transformation did not end the self. The soul that remembers is still a soul. Paul didn't have the word "soul" in the sense I use it. But he had the architecture, and he lived inside it.

Bonus: The neurological question

I'll add a fourth layer briefly, because it's honest to name it. What kind of experience was the Damascus road?

Paul describes it as a blinding light and a voice (we get this from Acts, but Paul confirms the visionary dimension in Gal 1:16 — God "revealed his Son in me"). The "in me" is important — this was not an external vision but an internal one. Paul's "thorn in the flesh" has been variously diagnosed: epilepsy, migraines, an eye condition, depression, PTSD from the persecution and the vision itself. Alan Segal read Paul through the lens of Jewish mystical traditions and suggested his experience fits the pattern of merkabah mysticism — visions of the divine throne-chariot.

I don't think the neurological question reduces Paul's experience or his theology. A brain event and a real encounter with the Ground are not mutually exclusive — on the consciousness-first frame, the brain is how a soul's experience appears from the outside. A visionary experience has a neurological correlate. That doesn't make it less real. It makes it real in the way all experience is real: as consciousness appearing within its own render.

What matters is what Paul did with the experience. He built communities. He wrote letters that have been read for two thousand years. He transformed a small Jewish sect into a world religion. Whatever happened on the Damascus road, it produced more than a neurological event. It produced a life.

Paul and your framework

If you're reading this as part of the Logos Christianity project, there are two things Paul gives you that nothing else in the tradition gives as clearly.

The indwelling axis, stated at full volume, before any Gospel. Galatians 2:20 is the single densest statement of the indwelling in the entire New Testament. "No longer I, but Christ" is the amnesia dissolving. "Yet I live" is the soul returning, transformed, still itself. Paul wrote this roughly twenty years before the Gospel of John was written. The Fourth Gospel is often credited with the indwelling theology — "abide in me as I abide in you." But Paul was already there. He had the architecture before John had the poetry.

The anti-monism railing, built into the same sentence. Paul knew that transformation could be mistaken for dissolution. He built the guardrail into the same clause: "yet the life I now live, I live." Not I vanish. Not I become God. Not I merge into the Ground and cease to be. I live — differently, from the Ground rather than from amnesia — but I live. The soul persists. That is the same guardrail the Eastern tradition spent centuries building. Paul built it in eight words.

The "in Christ" language as participation, not location. Paul's "in Christ" is the closest the New Testament gets to describing what the consciousness-first frame calls the soul's true environment: not a place but a medium, not membership but participation, not legal status but ontological reality.

Paul is not a systematic theologian. He's a pastoral writer — a man writing to specific communities about specific problems. But when you strip the letters down to their theological architecture, what's underneath is a participation-theology of the soul and the Logos that predates the entire tradition by decades.

Primary sources

[+]
The letters themselves

Read the seven undisputed letters in roughly chronological order: 1 Thessalonians (your earliest), 1 & 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, Romans, Philemon. Read each one in a single sitting — they're not long. Then read the disputed letters and notice what's different. Any modern translation is fine (NRSVue, ESV, NIV). The key is reading the letters as letters — addressed to specific people in specific situations — not as systematic theology dropped from heaven.

"Paul: The Man and the Myth" by Calvin Roetzel (Fortress Press, 1997) — a short, readable introduction that covers the historical Paul, the social world of his communities, and the Paul of Acts vs. the Paul of the letters.

Secondary scholarship
Where to go deeper

The New Perspective on Paul

E.P. Sanders, Paul and Palestinian Judaism (Fortress Press, 1977) — the book that changed everything. Dense, technical, not beginner-friendly, but foundational. If you can't read it, read the summary in: James D.G. Dunn, The New Perspective on Paul (Eerdmans, 2008), which collects his key essays and is much more accessible.

Paul the mystic

Alan F. Segal, Paul the Convert: The Apostolate and Apostasy of Saul the Pharisee (Yale University Press, 1990) — reads Paul's experience through the lens of Jewish mystical traditions and conversion psychology. One of the few books that takes the visionary experience seriously as experience.

Paul the participant

E.P. Sanders again: the "participation in Christ" reading of Paul. Also: Richard B. Hays, The Faith of Jesus Christ: The Narrative Substructure of Galatians 3:4-11 (Eerdmans, 2nd ed. 2002) — Hays argues that "faith in Christ" (pistis Christou) should be read as "the faithfulness of Christ," a participatory reading that shifts the entire axis of Paul's theology. Technical but worth the effort.

Paul and trauma

Paula Fredriksen, Paul: The Pagans' Apostle (Yale University Press, 2017) — a comprehensive and very readable treatment that takes Paul's Jewishness and his apocalyptic framework seriously. John G. Gager, Reinventing Paul (Oxford University Press, 2000) — a shorter, argumentative book that challenges the traditional reading from a psychological and historical angle.
