Maximus the Confessor Crash Course

David Jivan · davidjivan.net

If you have followed the arc this far — through Origen, condemned three hundred years after his death and recovered through fog; through the Cappadocians, who quietly carried theosis forward without anyone catching on; through Pseudo-Dionysius, whose apophatic theology survived because the church mistook him for one of Paul's converts — you are probably sitting with the same question I sat with for a long time. If the participation tradition really was the intellectual core of early Christianity, where did it go after 553? Did it only survive underground — borrowed names, unauthorized Latin translations, condemned syntheses, the line running through Eriugena to Eckhart? Or is there a stronger claim available somewhere — that it kept maturing right through the orthodox center, in the most canonical Greek-East line, never suppressed, never patched in later, formally taught?

The answer to that question is one person. And the strangest fact in patristic history is that the man the Byzantine state crippled in 662 wrote, posthumously and without revision, the conciliar definition that overruled the emperor who broke him.

Maximus the Confessor (c. 580–662) died in exile in Lazica — modern Tsageri, in the mountains of western Georgia — on 13 August 662. The empire had cut out his tongue and severed his right hand a few months earlier in Constantinople, in a public mutilation designed to silence the two organs of his theological work. He died of the injuries, in a province at the far edge of what was left of Roman power, after refusing to sign the emperor's preferred Christology. Nineteen years later, in 681, the Sixth Ecumenical Council — Constantinople III — adopted his exact technical distinctions, essentially verbatim, as the orthodox definition of how Christ wills. The emperor who had mutilated him was dead. The pope who had compromised with the imperial position was anathematized by name in the council's own acts. Maximus is the only patristic figure whose precise technical vocabulary became conciliar doctrine in his own terms within a generation of his death.

That asymmetry — mutilated by the state, canonized by the church, his terminology written into orthodox law — is the spine of everything else. It is the structural difference between Origen and Maximus. Origen got the participation tradition condemned. Maximus got the participation tradition canonized. The participation and apophatic axes were not only a condemned underground. They were also the formal teaching at the orthodox center, articulated by a man whose body the state could break but whose textual record it could not.

The contested life

For most of the last thousand years there was a settled biography. Maximus was born in Constantinople around 580 into a noble Christian family. He received a classical education, rose to become protoasecretis — chief imperial secretary — under the emperor Heraclius. Around 614 he abandoned the imperial court for the monastery of Philippicus at Chrysopolis, across the Bosporus. From Cyzicus he moved west under the Persian advance, eventually to North Africa by 626. That is the story the Greek Life tells. It was the standard account for centuries.

In 1973, Sebastian Brock published a Syriac Life of Maximus by one George of Resh'aina — a contemporary, hostile, Maronite source written within roughly a generation of Maximus's death (probably the 680s). The Syriac Life tells a different story. It says Maximus was born in Palestine, the illegitimate son of a Samaritan father and a Persian slave mother, raised by a monk at the monastery of Old Lavra near Jerusalem. The Greek Life, by contrast, is now thought to be no earlier than the tenth century — three hundred years after Maximus died. Recent scholarship has added a third possibility: an Alexandrian origin, hinted at in other early sources.

Origins are genuinely contested. Anyone who tells you flatly where Maximus was born is choosing one source and dismissing the others. The honest version is shorter: we don't know.

What we do know with confidence: by the mid-620s he was in North Africa. He was formed by Sophronius — the future Patriarch of Jerusalem who would surrender the city to Caliph Umar in 638. He spent the next two decades in Carthage writing the bulk of his theological corpus. He debated the former Patriarch Pyrrhus publicly at Carthage in July 645, with the African exarch Gregory presiding, and won the debate so completely that Pyrrhus publicly converted. He went to Rome by 646, co-orchestrated the Lateran Council of 649 with Pope Martin I — the first papal council ever convened without imperial authorization — and was arrested with the pope in 653. The biography is solid from his late forties onward. Before that, it dissolves into competing hagiographies.

One date worth holding. The hostile Syriac Life was not published in a modern critical edition until 1973. The complete Ambigua in English did not exist until Constas's Dumbarton Oaks edition of 2014. The complete Questions to Thalassios in English did not exist until Constas's Catholic University of America Press edition of 2018. For a figure this central to the Eastern church, that is genuinely unusual. The Maximus we can now read is a Maximus that was not available to scholars two generations ago. The recovery is happening in real time.

What monothelitism actually was

It is easy to read the seventh-century Christological disputes as theology students fighting over abstractions. They were not. They were arguments about who owned the church, fought during the worst geopolitical crisis the Eastern Roman Empire had ever faced.

The setup: the Council of Chalcedon (451) had defined Christ as one person in two natures, fully divine and fully human. A large portion of the Eastern church — particularly in Syria and Egypt — refused that formula. They were not Nestorians. They were not denying Christ's divinity. They held a variant called miaphysitism: one nature, formed of two. By the early seventh century, Chalcedonians and miaphysites had been excommunicating each other for nearly two hundred years. Egypt and Syria were drifting away from Constantinople along confessional lines.

Then came catastrophe. The Sasanian Persians invaded. Jerusalem fell in 614. The True Cross was carried off. Egypt fell in 619. Heraclius, by what looked at the time like a miracle, recovered everything by 629 — and then, within a decade, lost it all permanently to a different power: the Arab armies of the early caliphate took Damascus in 635, Jerusalem in 638, Alexandria in 642. Syria and Egypt were gone for good.

Inside that window — between the Persian recovery and the Arab conquest — Heraclius and his Patriarch Sergius engineered a unity-of-empire theology. If the Chalcedonians and miaphysites could be reconciled, the eastern provinces might hold. The first attempt was monoenergism: Christ has two natures but a single divine energy. When that drew resistance, they fell back to a softer version: Christ has two natures but a single will (monothelitism). Heraclius issued the Ekthesis in 638. His grandson Constans II issued the Typos in 648 — an imperial edict forbidding anyone, on pain of severe penalty, to discuss whether Christ had one will or two. Silence by decree.

This was not a grass-roots heresy. It was a state policy. And the resistance — Maximus, Sophronius before him, Pope Martin I alongside him — was simultaneously a doctrinal argument and a political one: the emperor does not get to define doctrine. Phil Booth's Crisis of Empire (UC Press, 2014) is the current scholarly synthesis here. The fight over Christ's will was a fight over who owns the church. When Maximus was finally arrested and tried in 655, the formal charges were treason, not heresy. The empire could not afford to admit it was killing the theologian for what he taught. It built a political case instead. I read the treason framing as a tell. Power that has to lie about why it is destroying you knows it cannot survive telling the truth.

The architectural moves

Six moves hold the system together. They are not separate doctrines stacked beside each other. They are one architecture viewed from different angles. I want to walk them slowly, because each one has direct bearing on the through-line of this whole project.

1. The logoi doctrine

Ambiguum 7 contains the sentence everything turns on: The one Logos is many logoi, and the many [logoi] are one [Logos] (PG 91, 1081C; Constas DOML vol. 1). Every created thing has a logos — an inner principle, a divine intention, an end — that exists eternally in the one Logos. The logoi are not Platonic forms floating in a separate intelligible world. Maximus says it directly in the same Ambiguum: the logoi of all things, immovably fixed in God, are what Holy Scripture calls predeterminations and divine wills (theia thelemata). The logoi are God's willings — not abstract patterns God consults but God's own intentions for each particular. To exist as a particular creature is to participate, at one's own logos, in the one Logos. To become what one is is to actualize that logos — to come into conscious accord with the divine intention one already structurally is.

Structurally, the logoi doctrine is a Level 1 claim — a claim about how the Logos articulates itself into the render-order, into the manifold of created things. The Dionysian apophatic God sits prior to the logoi. The logoi are how God appears as a structured render. I read this as the patristic version of the move the consciousness-first frame is making: the world is the rational self-articulation of one ground, and what looks like separate things are intentions of that ground individuated under the conditions of the render. The QFT-side version of this gets stated in There are no particles; this is the indwelling-side vocabulary for the same architecture.

Once the logoi doctrine is on the page, the participation axis stops looking like a pious metaphor and starts looking like a fully worked-out metaphysics. The one Logos is many logoi. Each created thing is a finite expression of an infinite center prior to which nothing else stands. The path of return is not an ascent toward something distant. It is the alter's recognition of the logos it already is.

2. The inversion of Origen

This is the move that lets the participation tradition survive Justinian's 553 condemnation of Origenism. Origen's system — at least as the sixth-century condemnations reconstructed it — ran stasis → kinesis → genesis. Rational souls began in a pre-existent rest with God, fell into motion through satiety or distraction, and ended up in bodies. The condemnation hit precisely this: the doctrine of pre-existent souls.

Maximus inverts the order. Genesis → kinesis → stasis. Creation first — rational creatures are made, not pre-existent. Then natural movement toward God — what creation is for. Then rest as the eschatological end, the consummation of the journey rather than its starting point. Polycarp Sherwood established the reading in The Earlier Ambigua of Saint Maximus and his Refutation of Origenism (Studia Anselmiana 36, Rome, 1955); it has held in subsequent scholarship.

I read this as the patristic template for a move I make a lot: locate what everything depends on in a condemned thinker, keep that, change the direction of travel so the condemnation no longer applies. The participation tradition is preserved. The heterodox conclusion is dropped. The empire's lawyers cannot prove the dependence. You can do this if you understand the architecture well enough to know where the load actually sits. Maximus is the figure who shows the move is available.

3. The five divisions and Christ's reverse mediation

Ambiguum 41 maps the structure of created reality through five divisions, each one a mediation gap that needs unification:

Division 1
Uncreated and created. The fundamental ontological division between God and everything else.
Division 2
Intelligible and sensible. Within creation, between purely spiritual and material.
Division 3
Heaven and earth. Within the sensible, between celestial and terrestrial.
Division 4
Paradise and inhabited world. Within the earth, between the original sanctuary and the fallen rest.
Division 5
Male and female. Within humanity, the division of the sexes.

The human person, standing in the middle, is a kind of natural bond mediating between the universal extremes through his parts, and unifying through himself things that by nature are separated (Constas trans.). The vocation of the human is to mediate the divisions. What Adam was meant to do and didn't, Christ does — in reverse order, in the actual Gospel narrative. Christ unifies male and female by being born of a virgin without ordinary sexual generation. Christ unifies paradise and world through cross and resurrection (the thief beside him). Christ unifies heaven and earth in the ascension. The entry through the heavens with the glorified body unifies sensible and intelligible. The offering to the Father unifies created and uncreated. The literal Gospel narrative is the metaphysics. The cosmology is not illustrated by the gospel; the gospel is the cosmology.

This is one of the more elegant answers I have seen to a question the audience for this work raises constantly: what does the historical Jesus actually do in a Logos framework where the Logos is everywhere? Maximus's answer is that the historical incarnation is not the Logos's first or only contact with the cosmos. The Logos has always been the rational principle of every created thing through the logoi. The incarnation is the moment at which the work of mediation Adam was meant to do is done from inside the divisions themselves — not as an exception to the cosmology, but as its consummation. Lars Thunberg's Microcosm and Mediator (2nd ed., Open Court, 1995) is the classic study; Emma Brown Dewhurst's "The Divisions of Nature in Maximus' Ambiguum 41?" (Studia Patristica 75, 2017) is the most useful recent treatment.

4. The thickening of the Word

Ambiguum 33 contains a passage I keep returning to. Maximus says the Word thickens (Greek pachynetai) in three modes at once: in the flesh of Jesus, in the letters of scripture, in the logoi of created things. The Word is thickened in syllables and letters... and conceals himself in the logoi of created beings... and is gradually disclosed (PG 91, 1285C–1288A; Constas). This is not three incarnations. It is one incarnation in three modes of presence. The same Logos at three densities.

What I read this as: the Christian claim that the Word became flesh is not a single anomalous event. It is the most concentrated instance of something the Word is always doing — disclosing itself into the conditions of finitude so it can be encountered there. The fleshly mode is the densest, but the textual mode and the cosmological mode are not categorically different. One thread among several: that is how the historical Jesus, the canonical text, and the rational structure of the world relate. Not three separate things. One self-disclosure, three densities. If you have read "The Logos was toward God", this is what is sitting under the move I make there about how to read the Fourth Gospel's prologue.

5. The natural will and the gnomic will

This is the Christological distinction that got him mutilated. Maximus distinguishes:

Natural will (thelema physikon): the will that belongs to a rational nature as such — the built-in orientation of any rational creature toward its own good. A horse has a natural orientation toward grass; a human has a natural orientation toward truth, goodness, beauty, God. This is structural, not deliberative. It belongs to the nature, not the person.

Gnomic will (thelema gnomikon): the will that deliberates under uncertainty. The gnomic act is what a rational creature does when it cannot see its own good clearly and has to weigh options, consider alternatives, decide between competing apparent goods. This is the everyday register of human willing — the experience of choice under ignorance.

Christ, on Maximus's account, has two natural wills — one belonging to his divine nature, one belonging to his human nature, both oriented toward God by their structure. And Christ has no gnomic will, because the gnomic will is what creatures do when they have forgotten their logos. Christ has not forgotten. His human nature wills its good directly because his human nature is not in the condition of amnesia.

Here is the structural overlay I read this through. I keep this marked as a reading, not as Maximus's claim. The gnomic will, on the consciousness-first frame, is the will of the alter who has forgotten its logos — the deliberative weighing that comes from not seeing what one already is. The natural will is what would orient the alter without obstruction if the amnesia were dissolved. Maximus's technical Christology is, structurally, giving vocabulary to a move the rest of this work makes in different language. Maximus would not put it this way. But the gap between natural and gnomic willing maps cleanly onto the gap between living from the render and remembering God. I read the patristic vocabulary as one available shape for the amnesia↔remembering axis. One thread among several.

If that mapping is right, then theosis — becoming Christlike — is exactly the process by which the gnomic will is progressively healed into the natural will. To remember the logos one already is, is to stop having to deliberate between alternatives one has lost the criterion for. Demetrios Bathrellos's The Byzantine Christ (Oxford Early Christian Studies, OUP, 2004) is the standard scholarly treatment of the historical-doctrinal version.

6. The "tantamount" theosis

In Ambiguum 7, Maximus writes that the Logos will divinize human nature without changing it into the divine nature, and will raise it up for His own sake to the same degree as He lowered Himself for man's sake. The structural keyword is "to the same degree." Theosis is not partial, not asymmetric. The depth of the descent equals the height of the ascent. Whatever the incarnation actually did, theosis is its mirror image.

He says it more flatly elsewhere in the same Ambiguum: Man is made god by divinization, and God is made man by hominization. The two halves of the formula are deliberately parallel. Theosis is not the human being becoming like God in a metaphorical sense. It is — on Maximus's bet — the human being raised into the same kind of relation that the incarnation established the other way.

And then, in Ambiguum 31, Maximus puts up a railing against the most obvious misreading: He completely divinized us, without in any way violating or essentially altering our nature. Theosis is not pantheism. The created does not collapse into the uncreated. The participation is real and is held under a distinction that does not dissolve. This is what scholars now call the essence–energies distinction in seed form: union with God is union with the divine energies (the operative self-disclosure of God), not union with the divine essence (which remains apophatically prior to all participation). Whether Gregory Palamas in the fourteenth century is unfolding what is already there in Maximus or whether he is reading his own scheme back into Maximus is a genuine scholarly question. Jean-Claude Larchet (La divinisation de l'homme selon saint Maxime le Confesseur, Cerf, 1996) reads it as already there; David Bradshaw and Bruce Foltz read Palamas as the innovator. Hold this open.

What he refuses to close

There are at least three questions Maximus deliberately leaves open. These are the places to slow down — the places where he holds himself back, where the text refuses to settle. The honest version of his thought includes the silences.

Was the incarnation contingent on the fall?

Question to Thalassios 60 reads the "mystery hidden from the ages" of Colossians 1:26 (note: Colossians is among the Pauline letters whose authorship is disputed; most scholars lean against Pauline authorship — though the cosmic-Christ trajectory is consistent with the undisputed letters, especially 1 Corinthians 15:28) as God's eternal pre-determination to become incarnate. The incarnation would have happened even without the fall. Adam's sin shapes the mode of incarnation — the suffering, the cross — but not the fact of it. Christ comes because creation is for Christ, not because Adam needed rescue.

If this is right, then the soteriological frame for Christology is reversed. The cross is not the reason; the cross is the result. The reason is cosmological. This is the position John Duns Scotus would arrive at independently six centuries later in the Latin West. It is also where Jordan Wood pushes further in The Whole Mystery of Christ (Notre Dame UP, 2022): for Maximus, creation is incarnation, not merely for incarnation. Torstein Tollefsen (The Christocentric Cosmology of St Maximus the Confessor, OUP, 2008) and Eric Perl read Maximus more conservatively — creation is created for incarnation but is not identical with it.

This is a live scholarly contest. Both readings are defensible from the texts. I read Wood as picking up a thread that is genuinely there; whether it is the strongest reading or one thread among several is the live question. Wood's reading lines up with the consciousness-first frame more cleanly than the strict-distinction reading does, and it is academically respectable enough that the "degree, not kind" bet about the incarnation has a patristic-scholarly cousin to point at. The line that anchors it: For the Word of God wills always and in all things to accomplish the mystery of his embodiment (Amb. 7, PG 91, 1084CD). Read flat, this is the line that underwrites Wood's reading. Hold the contest open. Do not force a resolution.

Is apokatastasis still in here?

Maximus distinguishes three senses of apokatastasis: (1) the restoration of each soul through ordinary moral and ascetic life, (2) the restoration of human nature in the resurrection, and (3) the universal restoration of all rational creatures — the controversial Origenist sense that the 553 anathemas condemned. Maximus accepts the first two and elaborates them. About the third he writes, in Quaestiones et Dubia 19: a better and more secret explanation, which is kept in the minds of the mystics, but we, as well, will honour by silence.

I read this less as Maximus quietly affirming universal restoration than as Maximus deliberately constructing under the shadow of 553. He keeps the door open without saying the words that would get him anathematized. The "honor by silence" line is, on my reading, strategic — it acknowledges the doctrine exists, signals that wise readers know what it is, and refuses to confirm or deny in print. Hans Urs von Balthasar (Cosmic Liturgy, Ignatius Press, 2003) reads it this way; Ilaria Ramelli (The Christian Doctrine of Apokatastasis, Brill, 2013) reads it more maximally as quiet affirmation; Mark Chenoweth's St Maximus the Universalist? argues for it on more popular ground. Brian Daley (The Hope of the Early Church, CUP, 1991) reads Maximus more conservatively, as genuine non-commitment. Andreas Andreopoulos in the Oxford Handbook holds the question open.

The complementary line worth holding alongside: paraphrasing 1 Corinthians 15:28 in Q. Thal. 60, Maximus writes that God will be all in all, surrounding all and giving subsistence to all in himself, because no being will have any movement separate from him, and nobody will be deprived of his presence. And then, balancing it, in Ambiguum 65: He rightly renders not well-being but eternal ill-being, since well-being is no longer accessible to those who have placed themselves in opposition to it. The same divine presence is light to the open and burning to the resistant. The Eastern "river of fire" reading. Hold both. He keeps the question genuinely open.

Smaller open questions worth flagging

Whether the essence-energies distinction is already present in Maximus or is read back into him by Palamas in the fourteenth century (Larchet and Schneider see it; Bradshaw and Foltz are more cautious). Whether the natural-will/gnomic-will distinction is a full philosophical anthropology (Bradshaw) or a polemical narrow-context move (Bathrellos). Constas's translation of the Ambigua has been flagged by some scholars as occasionally inflecting toward a Neo-Palamite reading; note the warning when reading him alongside the older selective translations. Where Maximus was actually born — Constantinople, Palestine, or Alexandria — remains unsettled.

A crash course should mark these as the live questions they are. The framework is recoverable. The unresolved interior to the framework is part of what makes it recoverable.

Trial, exile, mutilation

In 649 Maximus and Pope Martin I held the Lateran Council in Rome — five sessions, 105 canons, an unequivocal condemnation of monoenergism, monothelitism, and both imperial documents that had imposed them. No prior emperor's authorization. This was the first papal council convened in defiance of imperial sanction. The emperor read it correctly, as treason. The pope read it correctly, as necessary.

In June 653 imperial troops entered the Lateran palace and arrested the pope. He died of mistreatment in exile in Crimea in 655. Maximus was arrested separately and brought to Constantinople for trial.

The first trial, in 655, was on charges of treason. The Relatio Motionis records the proceedings. The formal accusations were political: alleged collusion with the African exarch Gregory, who had revolted against the emperor in 646; alleged correspondence with Arab leaders during the conquest of Egypt; alleged stirring of opposition to imperial authority in North Africa and Rome. Heresy was the underlying offense everyone in the room understood. But the formal charges were treason. Power cannot afford to admit it kills for doctrine; it builds a political case instead.

Maximus was condemned, exiled to Bizya in Thrace, briefly returned to Constantinople, then exiled again to Perberis. In 662 he was brought back for a second trial, condemned more harshly, and the sentence was carried out. His tongue was cut out. His right hand was severed. Theophanes the Confessor, writing in the early ninth century, records the act flatly: he cut out the saint's tongue, which was wise in God's ways, and cut off his right hand, since Maximus... had written a great deal in opposition to the Emperor's impiety (Chronographia, AM 6160).

The mutilation was symbolically precise. Read this as overlay: the empire understood, accurately, that the threat was not the man's body but the channels of speech and writing it ran through. So it destroyed the channels. The two organs of his theological combat — the one that spoke against the Typos and the one that wrote against it — were ritually destroyed by the state that read him as a media operation it could not answer. Pope Benedict XVI in his 2008 General Audience read the mutilation the same way: as a state response to media it could not refute. The body was then sent eight hundred miles east to Schemarum in Lazica, where Maximus died on 13 August 662. The text outran the body. The empire could break the organs of utterance. It could not break the network of copies.

Anastasius the Apocrisiarius — one of his disciples, mutilated alongside him, also exiled — wrote a letter around 665 describing the final months. It is the eyewitness account. The contemporary hostile Syriac Life by George of Resh'aina takes a different view: following the wicked Maximus, the wrath of God punished every place which had accepted his error — blaming the dyothelite theology for the Arab conquests. The same shape of "this heresy brought down the empire" move that institutional power has reached for in every century. State as fact; let it work.

The reversal

In 668 Constans II — the emperor who had ordered the Typos, who had Pope Martin arrested, who had Maximus exiled and mutilated — was assassinated in his bath at Syracuse. His son Constantine IV took the throne. By the late 670s the political situation had shifted: the Arab armies had stopped advancing, the eastern provinces were permanently lost, the unity-of-empire rationale for monothelitism had failed on its own terms. The reconciliation policy had not held the empire together. The provinces it was designed to retain had gone to Islam. Whatever monothelitism had been instituted to achieve, it had achieved none of it.

In November 680, Constantine IV convened the Sixth Ecumenical Council in Constantinople. It met for seventeen sessions over ten months. It read out the imperial documents Maximus had spent his life opposing, condemned them by name, and adopted, essentially verbatim, the dyothelite distinctions Maximus had defended in the Disputation with Pyrrhus and the Opuscula. Christ has two natural wills, one per nature; the human will is in voluntary subjection to the divine will, not absorbed into it; the union of natures is hypostatic, not at the level of will.

In Session XIII, on 28 March 681, the council anathematized by name the architects of the imperial position: Sergius, Pyrrhus, Paul, Peter (all patriarchs of Constantinople); Cyrus of Alexandria; Theodore of Pharan; and with them Honorius, who was Bishop of Rome, as having followed them in all things. Pope Honorius I (625–638) was condemned as a heretic by an ecumenical council the Catholic Church recognizes as valid. His successor on the throne of Peter, Pope Leo II, confirmed the condemnation in his ratification letter to the council. This is the textbook problem for papal infallibility, stated flatly in conciliar acts that papalencyclicals.net hosts in full.

Maximus's name does not appear in the council's positive list of fathers. He was not formally rehabilitated. He didn't have to be. His distinctions were the council's distinctions. His vocabulary was the council's vocabulary. The empire that had crippled him wrote his theology into orthodox law.

Both Eastern Orthodoxy and Roman Catholicism canonize him without reservation. His feast is 13 August, the day of his death. He is the only patristic figure whose technical Christological vocabulary became conciliar doctrine in his exact terms within a generation of his death.

"
Origen got the participation tradition condemned. Maximus got it canonized.
That is the structural difference between the two figures and the claim everything in this arc turns on. The condemned line survives through translation underground — Eriugena, Eckhart, the Latin-West channel. The orthodox line runs through the Greek East — Cappadocians, Pseudo-Dionysius, Maximus — and is canonized at the center of two of the largest churches in the world. The participation axis was not only an underground. It was made the formal teaching at the orthodox center, by the same imperial machinery that had killed its most articulate exponent nineteen years earlier.

Core reading list

The complete Maximus in English is genuinely recent. Before Constas's Ambigua in 2014 and his Questions to Thalassios in 2018, serious engagement required Greek or Eriugena's ninth-century Latin. Nine items in roughly the order of accessibility rather than chronology.

Click any entry to expand.

01
Four Hundred Texts on Love
Capita de Caritate · c. 626
[+]
What it is

Four centuries (one hundred chapters each) of short aphoristic sayings on love, written early in Maximus's monastic period, possibly as instructional material for a younger monk. The form is Evagrian — brief, gnomic, meant to be chewed slowly rather than read straight through. Theologically it covers passions, virtue, contemplation, and the soul's movement toward God, but the warmth is what matters here. The cosmic Christology is already present in compressed form. Love makes man god, and reveals and manifests God as man, through the single and identical purpose and activity of the will of both (First Century 27). Theosis in the warmer register.

Transmission & history

Complete in Greek. One of Maximus's most widely circulated works, particularly in the Byzantine monastic tradition. Translated by Polycarp Sherwood in 1955 (Ancient Christian Writers 21), again in Berthold's 1985 Classics of Western Spirituality volume, and included in volume II of The Philokalia (Faber, 1981).

What to look for

This is the right place to start with Maximus. Short, complete, devotional in register — and you can stop and start, which most of his corpus does not allow. First Century 8: The Logos of God, born once according to the flesh, always desires to be born spiritually in those who desire him. The continuous-incarnation thread is right there in compressed form. Read slowly. One century at a time.

George C. Berthold, trans., Maximus Confessor: Selected Writings (Classics of Western Spirituality, Paulist Press, 1985). Easiest single volume. Also: The Philokalia vol. II (Faber, 1981).
Reflection questions
  • The aphoristic form is meditative, not systematic. What does the form itself argue about what the spiritual life is and how it is known?
  • Maximus is drawing on Evagrius Ponticus, who would be partially condemned at the same 553 council that hit Origen. How does Maximus inherit the Evagrian tradition without inheriting what got Evagrius condemned?
  • The cosmic Christology and the devotional register are continuous in this work. Where does the seam show, if anywhere?
02
Mystagogia
On the Ecclesiastical Mystagogy · c. 628–630
[+]
What it is

A short, complete work — twenty-four chapters — reading the divine liturgy as a microcosm of the universe and a model of the human person. The famous formulation is here: the whole world, made up of visible and invisible things, is man; and conversely man, made up of body and soul, is a world (ch. 7). The church building, the liturgical action, the cosmos, and the human person are each isomorphic — the same structure at different scales. The liturgy is not a representation of salvation. It is salvation actually happening, at the scale where a particular community can participate in it.

Transmission & history

Complete in Greek, well attested in the manuscript tradition. The current best English is the 2019 Popular Patristics edition by Jonathan Armstrong with Shawn Fowler and Tim Wellings. Berthold's 1985 Classics of Western Spirituality volume also contains a complete translation.

What to look for

Chapters 1–7 establish the isomorphism between church, cosmos, scripture, and the human person. Chapter 7 has the microcosm formulation. Chapters 8–24 walk through the liturgy as the structural enactment. Read this if the systematic works feel too abstract — the Mystagogia is where the metaphysics lands in actual liturgical embodiment. After the Chapters on Love, this is the natural next step.

Jonathan J. Armstrong et al., trans., On the Ecclesiastical Mystagogy (Popular Patristics 59, SVS Press, 2019). Or Berthold's 1985 volume.
03
Disputation with Pyrrhus
Carthage, July 645
[+]
What it is

A transcript — or more likely a stylized literary reconstruction by Maximus or his circle — of the public debate held in Carthage in July 645 between Maximus and the deposed former Patriarch Pyrrhus of Constantinople. The African exarch Gregory presided. The format is genuine question-and-answer; Pyrrhus is not a strawman. This is the work in which the natural-will/gnomic-will distinction receives its clearest exposition. Pyrrhus publicly conceded the argument and reconciled with the Roman see at the end of the debate. He reverted to monothelitism within about a year under imperial pressure, but the transcript stands.

Transmission & history

Complete in Greek. The only full English translation is Joseph P. Farrell's 1990 edition (St. Tikhon's Monastery Press; reissued 2014). A note: Farrell's later trajectory has been controversial in Orthodox circles, and his theological commitments are visible in the introductory framing. The translation itself is serviceable. Until a different complete English translation appears, this is what exists.

What to look for

For the Christology, this is the single most useful text. Live debate is where Maximus's technical distinctions are at their clearest. If you want to understand what the Sixth Ecumenical Council was actually canonizing in 681, this is the source text. The pedagogy of the work is at the surface: definitions, distinctions, examples. If the Ambigua is the architecture, the Disputation is the working drawings.

Joseph P. Farrell, trans., The Disputation with Pyrrhus of our Father Among the Saints Maximus the Confessor (St. Tikhon's Monastery Press, 1990; reissued 2014).
04
Ambigua — 7, 10, 33, 41
Difficulties in the Fathers · selected
[+]
What it is

The Ambigua (literally "ambiguities" — difficult passages) are Maximus's extended philosophical reflections on contested texts from Gregory of Nazianzus and Pseudo-Dionysius. The corpus is large; do not start with the whole. The four to begin with: Ambiguum 7 (the logoi doctrine, the "tantamount" theosis formula, the Origen-inversion at its sharpest); Ambiguum 10 (the long meditation on contemplation, the modes of natural and supernatural knowledge); Ambiguum 33 (the thickening of the Word in flesh, scripture, and creation); Ambiguum 41 (the five divisions and Christ's reverse mediation).

Transmission & history

The Greek text survives intact. Eriugena translated the Ambigua to John into Latin in the 860s, which is how the Maximian logoi doctrine first entered the Western tradition. The complete English translation, by Nicholas Constas, did not appear until 2014 — the Dumbarton Oaks Medieval Library edition, two volumes. Before that, English readers had selections in Louth's 1996 Routledge volume and scattered pieces elsewhere; serious engagement required reading the Greek directly or working through Eriugena's Latin. Some scholars have flagged the introduction's Neo-Palamite framing — note the warning, then use the translation; it is the only complete one.

What to look for

In Amb. 7: read it twice. Once for the structure of the logoi doctrine, once for the theosis material at the end. In Amb. 10: the longest of the four and the most digressive in the ancient manner; skim and dwell. In Amb. 33: the pachynesthai passage on the three densities of the Word is the paragraph that matters most. In Amb. 41: the five divisions and the reverse-order Christ mediation. These four are enough for a serious working knowledge.

Maximos the Confessor, trans. Nicholas Constas, On Difficulties in the Church Fathers: The Ambigua (Dumbarton Oaks Medieval Library 28–29, Harvard University Press, 2014), 2 vols. The first complete English translation; essential edition. Andrew Louth's Maximus the Confessor (Routledge, 1996) includes Constas-independent translations of Amb. 1, 5, 10, 41, 71 and is a good cheaper entry point.
Reflection questions
  • Maximus says the one Logos is many logoi and the many are one Logos. Where does this differ from Platonic forms, and where does it differ from Stoic logoi spermatikoi? What makes it specifically Christian?
  • Amb. 41's five divisions are mediated by Christ in the actual narrative of the Gospels. Does that mean the Gospels are Maximus's metaphysics, or do they illustrate it?
  • Amb. 33 has the Word "thickening" in flesh, scripture, and the logoi of creation. Three modes of one self-disclosure. What kind of unity is being claimed there?
05
Questions to Thalassios — 60 and 43
on Col 1:26 · the mystery hidden from the ages
[+]
What it is

The Questions to Thalassios are sixty-five extended exegetical responses to questions about scripture, written for the Libyan abbot Thalassios. The format is question-and-answer; the content ranges from straightforward exegesis to some of the most ambitious metaphysical writing in the corpus. Question 60 is the one that matters: Maximus's reading of "the mystery hidden from the ages" (Col 1:26 — flag that Colossians is among the disputed Pauline letters, with most scholars leaning against Pauline authorship) as God's eternal pre-determination to become creation. Christ would have come even if Adam had not sinned. Question 43 contains the three-senses-of-apokatastasis passage where he refuses to teach what would have gotten him anathematized.

Transmission & history

Greek text survives. The complete English translation, by Maximos Constas, appeared in 2018 in the Fathers of the Church series. Before that, only selections existed in English. Blowers and Wilken's On the Cosmic Mystery of Jesus Christ (SVS Press, 2003) includes Question 60 and remains the best single-volume cosmic-Christ entry point.

What to look for

Question 60 first. If creation exists for the sake of incarnation, then Christ is not soteriological insurance against the fall — Christ is the reason there is a world at all. This is the line Jordan Wood reads as creation is incarnation and Torstein Tollefsen reads as creation is for incarnation. Hold that contest in mind as you read. Then Question 43 for the apokatastasis triple-sense — and notice what is missing in the third sense.

Maximos Constas, trans., On Difficulties in Sacred Scripture: The Responses to Thalassios (Fathers of the Church 136, Catholic University of America Press, 2018). The first complete English translation. Blowers and Wilken (SVS, 2003) for Q. Thal. 60 alone.
06
Two Hundred Texts on Theology
Capita Theologica et Oeconomica
[+]
What it is

Two centuries of theological-economical chapters — theologia in the patristic sense (knowledge of God in Godself) and oikonomia (the economy of God's action in creation and redemption). Denser and more philosophical than the Four Hundred Texts on Love. This is where the cosmic Christology emerges in compressed aphoristic form. The texts on the logoi, on the relation of time to eternity, on the seven and eight days, on prayer and theological knowledge — many of the architectural moves that get full treatment in the Ambigua show up here in distilled form.

Transmission & history

Complete in Greek. Included in volume II of The Philokalia. Best read alongside the Four Hundred Texts on Love — same register, more demanding work.

What to look for

The continuous-incarnation line in First Century 8 — The Logos of God, born once according to the flesh, always desires to be born spiritually in those who desire him — is the warm short form of what Ambiguum 33 does at length. After the Ambigua, this is where the theology lands in the prayer-life register.

The Philokalia, vol. II, eds. Palmer, Sherrard, Ware (Faber, 1981); also in Berthold's 1985 Selected Writings.
07
Documents from Exile
Relatio Motionis · Dispute at Bizya · eyewitness letters
[+]
What it is

Pauline Allen and Bronwen Neil's collection of the actual trial transcripts and exile-period letters. The Relatio Motionis is the record of Maximus's 655 trial; the Dispute at Bizya is the record of the 656 confrontation with Bishop Theodosius. The letters of Anastasius the Apocrisiarius and the Commemoration by Theodore Spudaeus give the eyewitness accounts of the mutilation and the death in Lazica. Not theology proper, but the historical bedrock on which everything else stands.

What to look for

If you only read the theological corpus you will not understand the cost. The trial transcripts, in particular, are striking: the prosecution does not actually argue theology. It tries to get Maximus to acknowledge that the emperor has the authority to define the doctrine. Maximus refuses, in real time, in primary documents you can read.

Pauline Allen and Bronwen Neil, eds., Maximus the Confessor and his Companions: Documents from Exile (Oxford Early Christian Texts, OUP, 2002).
08
Quaestiones et Dubia
short scriptural questions and difficulties
[+]
What it is

A collection of shorter exegetical questions and answers, ranging across both Old and New Testament passages. The "honorable silence" passage on universal restoration is here, in Question 19. The form is less systematic than the Questions to Thalassios; the texture is closer to a working notebook of solved difficulties.

What to look for

Mainly for Question 19 and the apokatastasis material, but the whole collection gives a useful view of how Maximus's exegetical mind moves at smaller scales than the Questions to Thalassios.

Despina D. Prassas, trans., St Maximos the Confessor's Questions and Doubts (Northern Illinois UP / Cornell, 2010), based on Declerck's CCSG 10.
09
Opuscula and Letters
not a reading — but know it exists
[+]
What it is

The Opuscula theologica et polemica are the technical Christological treatises — Opusculum 3 and Opusculum 6 on the natural and gnomic wills are the key ones for the dyothelite position. The Letters are a substantial body of correspondence, including the theologically dense Letter 2 to John the Cubicularius on love (available in Louth's 1996 volume).

The state of the field

No complete English translation of either the Opuscula or the Letters exists. Migne's Patrologia Graeca 91 (1865) remains the only complete edition; scholars still cite "PG 91, [column]." Andrew Louth's Maximus the Confessor (Routledge, 1996) includes the first English translation of Letter 2 and partial Opuscula material. The gap in the English corpus here is real. Until someone finishes the project, the Opuscula and Letters are accessible mainly through secondary scholarship.

Secondary guides

Seven books and a handbook. Read them alongside, not instead of, Maximus.

Maximus the Confessor
Andrew Louth · Routledge Early Church Fathers, 1996
The best single-volume English introduction. Louth gives a clean biographical sketch, situates the corpus, and provides his own translations of Letter 2, key Ambigua, and selected Opuscula. If you read only one secondary book, read this. Slim enough to read in a week, substantial enough to be the map you keep coming back to.
Cosmic Liturgy: The Universe According to Maximus the Confessor
Hans Urs von Balthasar · Ignatius Press, 2003 (orig. German 1941/1961)
The book that brought Maximus back into twentieth-century theological conversation. Balthasar reads him as a cosmic-Christ theologian whose system holds together at every scale. The "honorable silence" reading of the apokatastasis material is here. Deeply Catholic, deeply ambitious, occasionally tendentious — and indispensable.
Microcosm and Mediator: The Theological Anthropology of Maximus the Confessor
Lars Thunberg · Open Court, 2nd ed. 1995 (orig. Lund 1965)
The classic study of Maximus's anthropology and the five-divisions cosmology of Ambiguum 41. Thorough, philosophical, dense. If the architectural moves are what you want to understand, this is the book that walks you through them. Thunberg's Man and the Cosmos (SVS Press, 1985) is a more accessible distillation.
The Byzantine Christ: Person, Nature, and Will in the Christology of Saint Maximus the Confessor
Demetrios Bathrellos · Oxford Early Christian Studies, OUP, 2004
The standard study of the natural/gnomic will distinction and the dyothelite position. If you want to understand exactly what the Sixth Ecumenical Council was canonizing in 681 and why the distinction matters, this is the book.
The Christocentric Cosmology of St Maximus the Confessor
Torstein T. Tollefsen · Oxford Early Christian Studies, OUP, 2008
The careful philosophical study of the logoi doctrine. Tollefsen reads Maximus as keeping a strict Creator/creature distinction — the conservative position in the current scholarly contest. Read alongside Wood (below) to see the contemporary debate at full stretch.
The Whole Mystery of Christ: Creation as Incarnation in Maximus Confessor
Jordan Daniel Wood · Notre Dame UP, 2022
The radical reading of Maximus, recent and contested. Wood argues that for Maximus, creation is incarnation — to become God you become Christ, not Christlike. This is the closest existing patristic-scholarly position to a consciousness-first reading of the tradition. Tollefsen and Perl push back hard. Read both; do not force a resolution; the contest is itself instructive.
Crisis of Empire: Doctrine and Dissent at the End of Late Antiquity
Phil Booth · UC Press, Transformation of the Classical Heritage 52, 2014
The current scholarly synthesis on the political theology of the monothelite crisis. Booth places Maximus inside the seventh-century imperial collapse and reads the dyothelite resistance as inseparably theological and political. If the question is why the empire mutilated him and why the next council reversed itself, the answer is in this book.
The Oxford Handbook of Maximus the Confessor
Pauline Allen and Bronwen Neil, eds. · OUP, 2015
The current state-of-the-art reference handbook — forty chapters by specialists across every aspect of the corpus. Use it to triangulate when you have a specific question. Andreas Andreopoulos's chapter on eschatology is particularly useful for the apokatastasis question.

For further specialization: Jean-Claude Larchet, La divinisation de l'homme selon saint Maxime le Confesseur (Cerf, 1996) for theosis as architectural seed of Palamism; Adam G. Cooper, The Body in St. Maximus the Confessor (OUP, 2005) for the anti-Origenist anthropology of embodiment; Paul Blowers, Maximus the Confessor: Jesus Christ and the Transfiguration of the World (OUP, 2016) for a recent comprehensive treatment; Polycarp Sherwood, The Earlier Ambigua of Saint Maximus and his Refutation of Origenism (Studia Anselmiana 36, Rome, 1955), still the classic on the Origen-inversion.

Suggested reading path

Maximus is harder than Origen. His syntax is denser, his architecture is more compressed, and the apparatus of editions is younger. Start short. Start devotional. Get used to his voice before the architectural works. The defensible core — enough to have a genuine working knowledge of Maximus — is roughly 600–800 pages of primary text. That is a couple of months of serious reading at a sustainable pace. The complete Ambigua is a later project.

01
Four Hundred Texts on Love
Start here. Short numbered chapters, devotional in register, the cosmic Christology already present in compressed form. Read slowly, a century at a time. Berthold's 1985 volume is the cleanest single English edition. You can also use Philokalia vol. II.
~60 pages
02
Mystagogia
Twenty-four chapters, complete, on the cosmic-ecclesial-anthropological isomorphism. The microcosm formulation in chapter 7. The liturgy as the structural enactment of the cosmology. Armstrong et al. 2019 Popular Patristics edition is the current best.
~80 pages
03
Disputation with Pyrrhus
The Christology in real-time exposition. The natural-will/gnomic-will distinction at its clearest. Farrell's translation is what exists; use it. Read this before the Ambigua so the technical Christological vocabulary is already familiar.
~100 pages
04
Selected Ambigua: 7, 33, 41 (then 10)
The architectural core. Amb. 7 for the logoi doctrine and the "tantamount" theosis. Amb. 33 for the three modes of incarnation. Amb. 41 for the five divisions and Christ's reverse mediation. Amb. 10 for the longest sustained meditation on natural contemplation, last. Constas's two-volume DOML edition is the only complete English. The full Ambigua is a later project; these four are the necessary core.
~250 pages (these four)

Question to Thalassios 60 + Question 43

After the theology, the cost. The Relatio Motionis, the Disputation at Bizya, Anastasius's eyewitness letter. Allen and Neil 2002. Read this last. The Christology is what was being defended; this is what defending it looked like in practice.
~200 pages
07
Two Hundred Texts on Theology
The continuous-incarnation line ("the Logos always desires to be born spiritually") and the theology in the prayer-life register. In Philokalia vol. II, or Berthold.

Synthesis & closing questions

Reading Maximus from inside the Logos tradition is different from reading him as a historical subject. His system is recoverable, in the same sense Origen's is — the texts exist, the architecture is intact, the moves can still do work. But Maximus offers something Origen does not: the same participation tradition, with the radical universalist conclusion held under "honorable silence," matured through the orthodox center rather than recovered from the margins.

The questions below are not review questions. They are meant to pull threads that run from his work into the larger inquiry. Hold them open. These are the kind of questions that do not resolve on the first pass, and shouldn't.

questions to carry forward
On the two axes
The Logos tradition has a cosmological axis (how the Logos structures the render) and an indwelling axis (how the Logos inhabits the alter and is participated). Maximus holds both — the logoi doctrine as the cosmological move at Level 1, the theosis tradition as the indwelling axis. The two-axis structure is finally fully on the page in him. If both are one Logos, what does it mean that the same architecture answers what reality is and where I am in it?
On the logoi at Level 1
If the logoi are God's divine willings — God's intentions for each particular — then the structure of the render is not impersonal law. It is the trace of intention. The quantum field as the substrate of all physical excitation maps at Level 1 onto the logoi doctrine; every particular thing is a particular willing of the one Logos. What changes when the field is read this way? What stays the same?
On the gnomic will as amnesia
The gnomic will is the deliberative will of the creature who cannot see its logos clearly. The natural will is what would orient the creature without obstruction if the amnesia were dissolved. I read this as patristic vocabulary for the amnesia↔remembering axis — for the gap between living from the render and remembering God. Maximus does not put it this way. Is the mapping clean, or am I forcing it?
On Wood's reading
Jordan Wood reads Maximus as holding that creation is incarnation — to become God you become Christ, not Christlike. Tollefsen reads creation as for incarnation. The texts support both readings. The first lines up more cleanly with a consciousness-first frame; the second is the older mainstream. What is at stake in the choice? Where does the framework hold either way, and where does it break?
On the silence about apokatastasis
Maximus distinguishes three senses of universal restoration, accepts two, refuses to develop the third. He honors it by silence. Balthasar and Ramelli read the silence as a strategic concealment of universalism. Daley reads it as genuine non-commitment. The line is real; what to make of it is contested. If consciousness is God and cannot be permanently extinguished, what does the "honorable silence" cost — and what does it preserve?
On Pope Honorius
Pope Honorius I (625–638) was anathematized by name as a monothelite by the Third Council of Constantinople (Session XIII, 28 March 681), and the anathema was confirmed by Pope Leo II in his ratification letter. A Roman pope was condemned for heresy by an ecumenical council that the Catholic Church still recognizes as valid. This is the textbook problem for the doctrine of papal infallibility, and it is sitting in the same conciliar acts that canonized Maximus's terminology. State that flat. The reader can decide what to do with it.
On what canonization means
The empire mutilated him in 662. The empire's next ecumenical council adopted his exact terminology in 681. Both Eastern Orthodoxy and Roman Catholicism canonize him without reservation. The participation tradition is not the underground — it is the formal teaching at the orthodox center. What does it cost the institutional argument-from-tradition that the institution killed the theologian whose vocabulary it then made law?
On what becomes available
If the participation tradition was the formal teaching at the orthodox center, articulated by a canonized saint, written into ecumenical conciliar doctrine in his own terms — then the recovery argument this whole project depends on is not a recovery of a condemned underground. It is a recovery of what was openly taught, openly canonized, and then quietly buried by the popular reception of Christianity over the next thousand years. The texts are not lost. The English translations are recent enough that the body of work this project is reading is being read for the first time, in real time. What does it mean to recover what was never lost, only forgotten?

The thread that runs through all of it: Maximus bet his life on the claim that the Logos — the one rational principle in which all things subsist — is not the emperor's to define, and that the indwelling of that Logos in created things is not metaphor but the actual structure of what reality is. He died for the first half of that claim. The second half was canonized within a generation of his death.

The participation tradition was not preserved despite power. It was preserved at the cost of power eventually losing the argument. The body died at Schemarum. The text is still being recovered — in editions younger than most graduate students. That gap, between the silenced organs and the surviving network, is the structural fact about how a tradition outlives the regime that tries to break it.

That is where I have gotten with him. I do not know how it lands for you. But the texts are now in English, the synthesis is on the page, and the conciliar acts are public record. The work has been done. What remains is whether the framework holds under the full weight of human experience — which is not settled by any council or any condemnation. It is settled, if at all, by living with it.

He left enough on the page to live with.
