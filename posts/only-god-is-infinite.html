Only God Is Infinite

David Jivan · davidjivan.net

A friend sent a voice memo last week. He'd been thinking about infinity, about number, about God. He's a serious person — reads widely, prays honestly, doesn't posture. The claim he landed on was this: only God is infinite. Creation is finite. Numbers are finite. That's the line. If you let infinity into creation, you blur the Creator/creation distinction, and you're on the road to pantheism.

He said it as a conversation-ender. The kind of sentence that's meant to settle the question and let everyone go home. I've heard it before — from pastors, from theologians, from people I respect. It has the shape of a guardrail: God is infinite, we are not, and infinity is what makes God God.

I want to agree with the sentence. And I want to show that the sentence, taken seriously, opens something larger than the person saying it usually intends.

I first ran into Cantor in a library in Austin, Texas. I was supposed to be working — I had a stack of client projects and a deadline — but I'd gone down a rabbit hole about infinity and couldn't get out. The distinction between the Absolute and the transfinite hit me like something physical. I remember closing the book and sitting there, staring at nothing, trying to figure out if what I'd just read meant what I thought it meant. It did. And it was going to take me years to work through the implications.

Two movements. The first is about infinity — what the word actually names — and it can be closed. The mathematics resolves it. The second is about Trinity — what the number three actually names — and it can only be opened. The register shifts at the hinge. Movement one resolves. Movement two inquires.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
movement one

Infinite is not one word

When someone says "only God is infinite," they're using "infinite" as though it names one thing — a single property God has and everything else lacks. One word, one sense, applied univocally across God and number and cosmos. But the philosophical tradition and the mathematics say otherwise. There are at least two categorically different things that both get called "infinite." The friend's argument runs on treating them as one. That's a univocation — and it manufactures a competition that doesn't exist.

This is not a modern complication. Aristotle denied completed actual infinities twenty-three centuries ago. The infinite, for Aristotle, is always potential — you can always add one more, but you never arrive at a completed totality. There is no largest number; the series doesn't close. This was the Western default for two millennia. The claim "numbers are finite" was already incoherent in the fourth century BCE.

Then Cantor came along and broke the question open.

Georg Cantor, in the 1880s, didn't just prove that infinite sets exist. He proved that there are different sizes of infinity — an infinite hierarchy of them — and then he drew a line that the theological tradition had never drawn with this precision. He distinguished two categorically different things that both carry the word "infinite."

God's infinity
The Absolute Infinite
Unincreasable. Not on the scale. Not a size, not a maximum, not the top of a series. Defined by what it is not — not increasable, not a completed set, not a member of any collection. Negative theology in mathematical dress. Infinitum aeternum sive Absolutum. Not the largest item on the list. The condition for any list at all.
Creation's infinity
The transfinite
Real, graded, increasable. A genuine hierarchy of infinite sizes. The natural numbers form one infinity. The real numbers form a strictly larger one. And there are infinitely many such levels stretching upward. Infinitum creatum sive Transfinitum. On the scale. Subject to analysis. Genuinely infinite — but categorically different from the Absolute.

These are not two sizes. They are two categorically different things wearing one word. The friend's syllogism — "only God is infinite; therefore number must be finite" — trades on treating them as one. Once you distinguish, the syllogism dissolves. The transfinite was never competing with God. It was never on the same scale.

The worry behind the friend's claim has a name: pantheism. If creation gets real infinity, the Creator/creation line blurs. God is no longer unique. This worry has teeth — it's exactly what Cardinal Franzelin raised when Cantor brought the distinction to the Vatican in 1886. Take it seriously.

But the two-infinities distinction dissolves it on its own terms. The transfinite was never on the same scale as the Absolute. Creation's infinity never crowded God. The worry was generated by the univocation — one word forced to name one thing — not by the mathematics. God is not the biggest item on the list. God is why there is a list.

Cantor knew this would be the objection. He sent his distinction to Franzelin — a Vatican theologian, a cardinal, a man whose job was to guard the line between Creator and creation. Franzelin raised the pantheism worry directly. Christmas Day, 1885. Cantor sharpened the distinction in reply. Four days later Franzelin accepted: "no danger for religious truths." The pantheism charge was withdrawn. Not because the cardinal was soft. Because the precision was real. The cardinal stands in for the friend — devout, rigorous, moved by the precision when it was stated carefully enough.

Whether the cosmos is physically infinite is a separate question. Physics has not settled it. Finite does not mean bounded — a closed curved space can be finite in volume with no edge. Cantor himself denied the universe is spatially infinite. The theological dissolution — infinity in creation does not crowd God because the infinities are categorically different — holds regardless. The physics question lives in a different lane.

So the friend is right — but the sentence means something more precise than intended. Only God is infinite in the absolute sense. Creation genuinely has the transfinite. These are not the same claim, and mixing them is where both pantheism and its denial go wrong. Movement one closes here.

The friend's voice memo has been sitting in my phone for weeks now. I've listened to it three times. He's a good man — serious, honest, someone who prays like he means it. And the thing he said — "only God is infinite" — is true in a way he didn't intend. The Absolute is not on the scale. It never was. But saying "only God is infinite" as a way to close the conversation is different from saying it as a way to open it. Movement one closed the conversation he wanted to close. Movement two is the one he didn't know he was opening.

"
The infinity question taught one habit: before measuring anything, ask what kind.
"What kind of infinite?" dissolved a false binary. Applied to another number Christianity has been arguing about, the same question — "what kind of three?" — opens something that movement one's precision cannot close.

The register shifts here. Movement one resolves. Movement two opens. What follows is softer ground — a bet, not a proof. The precision that closed the infinity question is the same precision that opens the Trinity question. But it opens it without closing it, and I want to be honest about that difference.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
movement two

What kind of three

Numeric three
Three persons
Three items of the same kind. Co-equal, co-eternal, distinct in relation, identical in substance. The Nicene settlement. A count. Father, Son, Spirit — each fully God, none reducible to either of the others. The friend means this three.
Structural three
Three levels
Three modes or aspects of a single reality. The Ground — what is, prior to structure. The ordering principle — the rational pattern through which everything coheres. The localized perspectives — the dissociated subjects who experience the pattern from the inside. Not a count but a topology.

The question is not whether God is three. It's what kind of three. And that question has a genealogy.

~50s–90s CE
NT triadic formulae
Paul's blessing: "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, the love of God, and the communion of the Holy Spirit" (2 Cor 13:14). The baptismal formula in Matthew 28:19. Liturgical patterns — triadic but not yet metaphysical. The Spirit barely individuated. These are doxologies, not ontology.
~180 CE
Theophilus of Antioch
First known use of the Greek trias. But his triad is God, Logos, and Sophia — not Father, Son, and Spirit. The three was already fluid before anyone fixed it.
~200 CE
Tertullian
Coins the Latin trinitas. Tres personae, una substantia — three persons, one substance. The formula that will dominate the West. But subordinationist: the Son proceeds from the Father and is not co-equal. The symmetry is not yet there.
~230 CE
Origen
Eternal generation — the Son is eternally begotten, not created. The deepest philosophical architecture anyone had yet built for the relationship. But graded: the Son is deuteros theos, a "second God." Richer than what replaced it. More honest about the asymmetry the later councils would smooth over.
325 CE
Nicaea
Homoousios — "of one substance." Anti-Arian: the Son is fully divine, not a creature. Settles that question. Does not yet articulate the full three-in-one. The Spirit is barely mentioned in the original creed.
~370s CE
The Cappadocians
Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nazianzus, Gregory of Nyssa. One ousia, three hypostaseis. This is the formulation that becomes orthodoxy — the co-equal, co-eternal, symmetric three. Worked out through genuine philosophical argument, not delivered as a given.
381 CE
Constantinople
The Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed. The Holy Spirit gets its clause: "the Lord, the Giver of Life, who proceeds from the Father." The symmetric, co-equal three-in-one is formally articulated — roughly 350 years after the first communities. This is the settlement. It was constructed, not revealed whole.

The genealogy shows that the symmetric co-equal Trinity was not delivered fully formed. It was constructed across three centuries, through genuine philosophical argument and political pressure, with real alternatives on the table at every stage. The structural, relational three hardened into the numeric, ontological three over time. That is not a scandal. It is history.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The foundational thing for the earliest sophisticated thinkers was not symmetric eternal threeness. It was the Logos.

The Author of the Fourth Gospel opens not with Father, Son, and Spirit but with the Logos — the rational ordering principle of reality. Justin, Clement, Origen built their systems around the Logos as the primary category. The three-persons formulation came later, and it came through argument, not through reception of a self-evident truth. The pre-Nicene tradition was Logos-first. The three-persons architecture was the settlement that eventually replaced it — more symmetrical, more politically useful, less philosophically textured.

The claim I think you can make, if you've read the genealogy honestly: the coequal immanent Trinity is a fourth-century settlement, not a self-evident bedrock. The pre-Nicene tradition had richer, more asymmetric architectures — and the Logos sat at the center of all of them.

The full Logos lineage is traced in The Logos lineage. The reading that cracked the prologue open is in The Logos was toward God.

The orthodox counter is straightforward: development is not invention. The Trinity was implicit in the NT triadic formulae; the councils clarified what was already there. The acorn was always an oak. This is a serious position — Newman's development of doctrine is its formal version — and I'm not going to pretend I've demolished it. I disagree with it, but the disagreement is honest, and the counter deserves to stand.
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Where I've landed — and I want to be clear this is a bet, not a deduction.

The Logos-first architecture is, I think now, more fundamental than the three-persons architecture. The three-level topology — Being as the irreducible ground, the Logos as the rational ordering principle through which everything coheres, individual subjects as dissociated perspectives experiencing the pattern from the inside — is, on this reading, a more precise description of the structure the Trinity was trying to articulate. Not the same claim. A different architecture, built from different commitments.

This is heterodox. I know. The Cappadocian formula is orthodoxy. What I'm proposing is closer to what Eckhart called the Gottheit — the Godhead prior to the Trinity, the desert in which all distinctions dissolve — and what the consciousness-first commitment points toward: if awareness is fundamental and not produced by matter, then the deepest level of reality is not three persons in relation but the undifferentiated ground in which all structure, including the Logos, appears. Eckhart was condemned for this. I know where I'm standing.

CONTESTED. This reading diverges from Nicene orthodoxy. The three-level topology maps the structure of reality, not the inner life of God as three co-equal persons. Calling it "the Trinity, understood correctly" would be dishonest. It is a different architecture, built from different commitments. I own that.

Movement two doesn't close. It opens. "What kind of three?" has a genealogy and a range of live positions. I've named where I stand without pretending the question is settled. The register asymmetry between the two movements is deliberate — one resolves, the other inquires.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Both movements share one shape: a quantity treated as bedrock, corrected by a kind-distinction the bedrock is prior to. "Infinite" looked like one thing; precision revealed two categorically different things. "Three" looks like one thing; the genealogy reveals it has been at least two — a numeric claim and a structural claim — since the beginning. In both cases, the precision costs something familiar and opens something more careful.

The friend's sentence is still ringing in my ear. Only God is infinite. I agree with it now more than I did before I ran it down — and it means something different than he meant when he said it. The Absolute is not on the scale. It never was. And the number of persons in God might not be the kind of question a count can answer.
