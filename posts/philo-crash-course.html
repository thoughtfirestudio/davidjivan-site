Philo crash course — a study guide

David Jivan · davidjivan.net

You cannot get from Heraclitus to the Gospel of John without passing through Philo of Alexandria. He is the hinge of the entire Logos tradition — a first-century Jewish philosopher who synthesized Platonic and Stoic philosophy with the Hebrew scriptures decades before the Fourth Gospel was written. His Logos theology gave the author of John the vocabulary for "In the beginning was the Logos." Without him, that opening sentence reads as poetry. With him, it reads as a precise metaphysical claim about the structure of reality.

Philo was born around 25 BCE into one of the wealthiest Jewish families in Alexandria — the intellectual capital of the Mediterranean world. His brother Alexander was one of the richest men in the city. His nephew Tiberius Julius Alexander would later become Roman prefect of Egypt and play a role in the siege of Jerusalem in 70 CE. Philo himself led the Jewish embassy to the Emperor Caligula around 40 CE, after anti-Jewish riots in Alexandria. He was a man of practical affairs, not only a philosopher. He died around 50 CE — before any Gospel was written, before Paul's letters were collected, before anything that would become the New Testament existed as a text. The direction of influence runs one way.

The synthesis

Philo's entire project can be stated in one sentence: Moses and Plato were saying the same thing. He spent his life demonstrating this — not as an apologetic trick but as a genuine philosophical conviction that the Torah and Greek philosophy were independent witnesses to a single rational structure underlying reality.

Alexandria made this possible. By the first century BCE, its library held hundreds of thousands of scrolls. Its Jewish community — one of the largest outside Judea — had already spent generations translating their scriptures into Greek (the Septuagint, begun around 250 BCE) and articulating their tradition in philosophical terms. Philo inherited this project and took it further than anyone before him.

He understood, with unusual clarity, that the God of Hebrew scripture — the God who says "Let there be light" and light appears, whose Word (dabar) creates — maps onto a philosophical category the Greeks had been developing for centuries: the Logos. The Stoics taught that the Logos was the rational principle pervading and organizing all of reality — what made the cosmos a cosmos rather than chaos. Plato taught that the sensible world is shaped according to eternal, intelligible Forms. Philo saw the Hebrew creation narrative — God speaking the world into existence — as pointing toward the same structure. And he had a ready tool for the synthesis: the Logos as the place where the Platonic Forms reside within God, as God's own rational self-expression toward creation.

This was not metaphor. He was solving a genuine philosophical problem. If God is truly transcendent — beyond all categories, all limit, all relationship to the finite — then how does God create? How does the One touch the many? The Logos is Philo's answer: an intermediary reality that is divine without being the ultimate God, that mediates without collapsing the distinction.

The Logos theology

Six concepts. Together they form the most architecturally complete Logos theology produced before Christianity.

01
The Logos as the intelligible world
"Plato had taught that reality has two levels: the intelligible world of eternal Forms and the sensible world of material things. Philo relocates the Forms. They are not freestanding eternal objects — they are God's thoughts, the content of the divine mind. The Logos is the \"intelligible world\" where these archetypal patterns reside before creation. \"The archetypal seal itself, by participation in which each thing has received its form, is itself the archetypal model, the idea of ideas, the Reason of God\" (On the Creation VI.25). Everything that has form, has it by participating in the Logos."
02
The Logos as instrument of creation
"God creating the world is like an architect designing a city. The architect first conceives the whole city in his mind — streets, buildings, proportions — as a coherent plan. Only then does he build in matter. The Logos is that plan: the divine Reason containing the totality of the Forms. When the Torah says \"God said, 'Let there be light,'\" Philo hears: the rational principle of light was expressed from the divine mind into material reality. \"God, not making use of anything else as a fellow-worker, in an incorporeal work, but His own Word alone, by which He also created the world\" (Allegorical Interpretation I.65). Almost verbatim what John 1:3 will say."
03
The Logos as God's face toward creation
"One of Philo's most important distinctions: God-as-He-is-in-Himself versus God-as-He-turns-toward-creation. God in himself is utterly beyond predication — beyond qualities, beyond space, beyond even \"existence\" as a category derived from created things. The Logos is God's self-expression toward the world — God's rational face, God's \"shadow\" and \"image.\" Not in the sense of being unreal, but as the precise form that something takes when it turns toward the light. When humans encounter rational order in the world — when they grasp a mathematical truth or perceive the lawfulness of nature — they are, on Philo's account, encountering the Logos. This is apophatic theology before the term existed."
04
"The Logos as \"firstborn Son of God\""
"Philo reaches for personal language when the abstract terminology starts to feel inadequate. He calls the Logos the \"firstborn son\" (prototokos) — the first and primary expression of the divine nature, prior to all created things, the eldest offspring of the divine mind. Not biological, not Trinitarian. The same Greek word that appears in Colossians 1:15 (\"the firstborn of all creation\") — and Philo writes it without any reference to Jesus of Nazareth, out of purely philosophical and scriptural reasoning about the divine nature."
05
The Logos as high priest and mediator
"The Logos stands \"on the confines of both\" — the threshold between God and creation, neither fully absorbed into the divine nor fully part of the created. \"This same Word is continually a suppliant to the immortal God on behalf of the mortal race, which is exposed to affliction and misery; and is also the ambassador, sent by the Ruler of all, to the subject race\" (Who Is the Heir 230-231). It moves in both directions: praying to God on behalf of humanity, bringing God's power and wisdom down to humanity. Priestly and intercessory language for what is otherwise a philosophical concept."
06
The Logos as the divider
"In Genesis 1, God separates: light from darkness, waters above from waters below, sea from dry land. Philo reads each act of separation as an act of the Logos — the principle of distinction and definition. \"He who divides all material things and gives them their proper boundaries is the sharp Logos of the everliving God\" (Who Is the Heir 205-206). Without the Logos, reality would be undifferentiated chaos. The Logos is what makes things be what they are — what gives each thing its particular form, its distinct nature. The Logos as the razor that carves reality into intelligible structure."

One more thing about the Logos that the conceptual list misses: Philo does not leave it static. In On Dreams (I.147) he describes the Logos moving "incessantly upwards and downwards through the whole of it, dragging it upwards along with them whenever they soar aloft." The Logos is not a frozen blueprint. It is the living, dynamic principle of cosmic animation — descending into creation, ascending back toward God, drawing creation along with it. This anticipates the Neoplatonic "procession and return" that would become central to Plotinus and later to Christian mysticism.

Philo and the author of John

Whether the author of John read Philo directly is disputed. The three scholarly positions are: direct literary dependence, common tradition (both drawing independently on Jewish Wisdom literature — Proverbs 8, Sirach 24, the Wisdom of Solomon), or general cultural diffusion (Philo's Logos vocabulary was so widespread in first-century Hellenistic Judaism that it could have been absorbed without reading Philo directly). The honest answer is that we cannot prove which is correct. But the question is less important than what either answer reveals.

Put the two side by side and the structural correspondence is immediate.

the parallels
"**Philo**"
"John"
The Logos is with God and distinct from God
"\"And the Logos was toward God\" (1:1b) — the preposition pros implies both distinction and intimacy"
The Logos is divine but not the ultimate God
"\"And what God was, the Logos was\" (1:1c) — identity of nature, not of person"
The Logos is the instrument of creation — all things made through it
"\"All things came into being through him, and without him not one thing came into being\" (1:3)"
The Logos as light — both physical and noetic, the illumination by which minds apprehend truth
"\"The true light, which enlightens everyone, was coming into the world\" (1:9)"
"The Logos as \"image\" or \"face\" of God in the world"
"\"The only-begotten Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, he has made him known\" (1:18)"

One substitution worth noting. The Jewish Wisdom tradition personifies Wisdom as feminine — Sophia in Greek, Hokhmah in Hebrew. Proverbs 8: "The Lord created me at the beginning of his work." Sirach 24: "I came forth from the mouth of the Most High." The Wisdom of Solomon: "a breath of the power of God, a pure emanation of the glory of the Almighty." This tradition already does most of the heavy lifting — a divine figure, pre-existent, present at creation, God's instrument in making the world. The author of John chose the masculine philosophical term logos over the feminine sophia, aligning with both Greek philosophy and the Hebrew dabar — God's speech as creative power. The author was writing for an audience that heard both channels.

Then comes the line Philo could never have written.

And the Logos became flesh and dwelt among us. — John 1:14

For Philo, the Logos mediates between the transcendent God and the material world precisely by remaining immaterial. If the Logos were to take on a particular body, in a particular place and time, it would cease to be the universal principle it must be to do its job. Incarnation would be a category error of the highest order. The divine principle of all creation cannot be located in one created thing without destroying what makes it the divine principle of all creation.

What the author of John was doing is therefore more precise and more audacious than it appears. He spent thirteen verses establishing the Logos in its full cosmic dignity — with God, divine, the principle of all creation, the light of all human minds. All the philosophical furniture of Philo's system, carefully assembled. And then: this cosmic principle took on flesh. Right here. In history. In a body.

The Prologue is not saying "Jesus was a good teacher with divine inspiration." It is using Philonic vocabulary to make the claim as sharp as possible: the very ground of reality, the rational structure of the cosmos, the firstborn of God — that entered history. Without Philo, you can read John 1 as a beautiful poem. With Philo, you read it as a philosophical detonation.

Core reading list

Six primary texts. The first is the essential starting point. The rest deepen the system from different angles.

01 On the Creation of the World +
What it is
"Philo's Genesis commentary and the clearest exposition of his Logos theology. He reads Genesis 1 as a philosophical account of how God creates through the Logos — first making the intelligible world (the blueprint containing all the Forms), then the sensible world (the material copy). The architect analogy is here: God conceiving the whole city in his mind before building it in matter. The Logos as \"the idea of ideas, the Reason of God\" is here. If you read one text of Philo's, read this one."
What to look for
"Sections IV-VI (16-25) contain the core Logos theology — the intelligible world located in the divine Reason, the architectural analogy, the \"idea of ideas\" formulation. Section XXIII (69) on the human mind as image of the Logos is the epistemological key: because the human mind is made in the image of the Logos, rational thought participates in the same principle that structures all of reality. The chain is explicit: God → Logos → human intellect. Watch also how Philo handles the \"days\" of creation — he does not treat them as temporal sequence but as logical priority. Day one is the creation of the intelligible world; the rest follow as its material unfolding."
"The Yonge translation (1854, public domain) is freely available online but archaic in language. David T. Runia, Philo of Alexandria: On the Creation of the Cosmos according to Moses (Brill, 2001) is the scholarly standard — includes Runia's detailed commentary. For the complete works: F.H. Colson and G.H. Whitaker, Philo, Loeb Classical Library (Harvard UP), 12 volumes — the standard critical edition with facing Greek."
Questions
  • Philo relocates the Platonic Forms from independent existence into the mind of God. What is gained by this move? What problems does it create — particularly around whether the Forms retain their necessity if they depend on God's will?
  • The architect analogy implies that the sensible world is a copy of the intelligible original. How does this map onto the three-level ontology? Is the intelligible world Level 1 (the render's rational structure) or something closer to Level 0?
  • Philo treats rational thought as participation in the Logos. If this is right, what does it mean for the status of scientific knowledge — is physics the Logos studying itself?
02 Who Is the Heir of Divine Things +
What it is
"Structured as a meditation on Abraham's prayer in Genesis 15, this treatise contains Philo's most celebrated description of the Logos as the boundary principle between God and creation. The Logos as \"divider\" — the razor that separates and defines all things — originates here. So does the Logos as mediator, standing \"on the confines of both,\" neither fully divine nor fully created, praying to God on behalf of humanity and bringing God's power to humanity. The most architecturally precise of Philo's works on the Logos's mediating function."
What to look for
"Sections 205-206 for the Logos as divider — the razor-sharp Word that gives all things their proper boundaries. Sections 230-231 for the Logos as mediator and \"suppliant\" — the most explicit priestly-intercessory text in Philo. The famous passage on the Logos as \"neither created nor uncreated\" (section 206) — standing between — is the theological claim that most directly anticipates the Christological debates of the 4th century. Philo did not have the Arian controversy in view, but he was already working on the problem it would eventually name."
"F.H. Colson and G.H. Whitaker, Philo, Loeb Classical Library, vol. IV (Harvard UP). Also in the Yonge translation (free online). Sections 205-231 are the essential core."
Questions
  • "The Logos as \"neither created nor uncreated\" — where does this land on the later Christian spectrum? Is this closer to Arius (the Logos as highest creature) or Nicaea (the Logos as fully divine)?"
  • Philo describes the Logos as the principle that makes things be what they are — the source of distinction and form. How does this compare with the Stoic account of the Logos as the rational principle immanent in all things? Where does Philo agree with the Stoics and where does he break?
  • "The mediator role — \"suppliant\" upward, \"ambassador\" downward — implies the Logos has something like agency. Is Philo's Logos personal? Or is the personal language just an unavoidable metaphor for something impersonal?"
03 On the Confusion of Tongues +
What it is
"Philo's reading of the Tower of Babel narrative, containing his most comprehensive single passage on the titles of the Logos. Section 146 is the passage most studied by scholars of early Christology: the Logos receives a cascade of names — \"the Beginning,\" \"the Name of God,\" \"the Word,\" \"man according to God's image,\" \"he that sees\" (Israel), \"eldest of the angels,\" \"archangel.\" This is almost a catalog of the titles early Christianity would apply to Christ — and Philo writes it without any reference to Jesus, out of purely philosophical and scriptural reasoning about the nature of the divine Word."
What to look for
"Section 146-147 is the essential passage. Read it slowly, then read the Prologue of John, then read it again. The resonance is structural, not incidental. Also section 41 — the Logos as \"eldest son\" and \"first-born\" (prototokos), the same Greek word that appears in Colossians 1:15. The Logos \"imitating the ways of his father, has formed such and such species, looking to his archetypal patterns\" — delegation of divine creativity, not diminishment. The Babel narrative itself matters less than the philosophical excursus it provokes."
"F.H. Colson and G.H. Whitaker, Philo, Loeb Classical Library, vol. IV (Harvard UP). Also in the Yonge translation (free online). Sections 41 and 146-147 are the core."
Questions
  • "Philo calls the Logos both \"eldest angel\" and \"firstborn Son of God\" — are these the same claim in different registers, or are they in tension? What does it mean to be both the cosmic mediator and an \"angel\" (messenger)?"
  • Read section 146 against John 1:1 and Colossians 1:15-20. The parallels are undeniable. Does the question of direct literary dependence actually matter, or is the structural convergence itself the significant fact?
04 On Flight and Finding +
What it is
"Philo's most extended cosmological treatment of the Logos, structured as a meditation on Hagar's flight (Genesis 16). Here the Logos appears as \"eldest Son\" and \"high priest\" in its fullest elaboration — mediating between God and creation in both directions, offering the created world back to God and bringing divine power down to the world. The high priestly language is more developed here than anywhere else in Philo's corpus."
What to look for
"Section 101 on the Logos as high priest — the priestly imagery applied to a philosophical concept. Section 109 where Philo calls the Logos \"the Son of God and Sophia\" — placing the Logos at the intersection of divine intellect and divine wisdom, explicitly connecting the masculine philosophical term with the feminine Wisdom tradition. This is the passage that most clearly shows Philo holding both channels open: Greek philosophy and Hebrew Wisdom, logos and sophia, as two names for one structure."
"F.H. Colson and G.H. Whitaker, Philo, Loeb Classical Library, vol. V (Harvard UP). Also in the Yonge translation (free online)."
Questions
  • "Philo calls the Logos both \"Son of God\" and \"Sophia\" — holding the masculine and feminine names together. The author of John chose logos over sophia. What was gained and what was lost in that choice?"
  • The high priest mediates by standing between. In the Logos tradition, standing between is not a weakness — it is the structural position that makes creation possible. What does this imply about the nature of mediation itself?
05 Allegorical Interpretation I-III +
What it is
"Philo's running allegorical commentary on Genesis 2-3 — not the cosmological creation account of Genesis 1 (that's On the Creation) but the garden narrative, Adam and Eve, the tree and the serpent. This is where you see Philo's method in action: how he reads Torah through philosophical categories. Each element of the narrative encodes a truth about the soul's relationship to God. Adam is the rational mind. Eve is sense-perception. The serpent is pleasure. The garden is the life lived according to the Logos. The expulsion is the soul's fall into sensory orientation."
What to look for
"Book I.65 — the Logos as God's sole instrument of creation (\"not making use of anything else as a fellow-worker\"). Book III.96 — the chain of participation made explicit: God → Logos (image of God) → creation (image of the Logos) → humanity (made in the image of the Logos, not directly of God). The method matters as much as the content. Philo's allegorical approach — reading narrative as encoding philosophical truth — passes directly to Clement of Alexandria, then to Origen, and through them to the whole Christian intellectual tradition. This is where it starts."
"F.H. Colson and G.H. Whitaker, Philo, Loeb Classical Library, vol. I (Harvard UP). Also in the Yonge translation (free online). Three books, manageable in length."
Questions
  • Philo's allegorical method assumes that the literal meaning of a text can be a shell around a deeper philosophical meaning. Where does this method open things up, and where does it risk becoming a way to avoid what the text actually says?
  • The chain God → Logos → creation → human mind implies that rational thought is always already participation in the divine. If this is right, what are the consequences for how we understand consciousness?
  • Origen will take Philo's allegorical method and build an entire hermeneutic on it. What in Philo's approach already contains the seeds of the later Origenist tradition?
06 On the Embassy to Gaius +
What it is
"The historical work — Philo's firsthand account of leading the Jewish embassy from Alexandria to the Emperor Caligula, protesting the anti-Jewish violence of 38 CE and Caligula's demand that his statue be installed in the Jerusalem Temple. This is Philo the political man, not the philosopher. The text gives you the person behind the system: a community leader putting his life on the line for his people, navigating Roman imperial politics with intelligence and courage. Caligula is portrayed as a buffoon and a tyrant; the Jews as dignified under pressure."
What to look for
"The portrait of Caligula's madness and the specific dynamics of the embassy audience. The contrast between Philo's philosophical composure and the terror of the situation. This text contains no Logos theology in the technical sense — that is precisely why it matters. It shows you that the man who built the most sophisticated Logos system in the ancient world was also a man who stood before an unstable emperor on behalf of his community. The philosophical work and the political courage are not separate. They come from the same person."
"F.H. Colson, Philo, Loeb Classical Library, vol. X (Harvard UP). Also in the Yonge translation (free online). A complete narrative — reads more like history than philosophy."
Questions
  • What does it mean for a Logos theologian to face political violence? Does the philosophical system inform how Philo handles the crisis, or are these separate domains for him?
  • "Origen's father died a martyr; Origen himself was tortured. Philo risked his life in the embassy. What does the pattern suggest about the relationship between Logos theology and personal cost?"

Secondary guides

Four books that provide the scholarly infrastructure for reading Philo seriously. Read them alongside the primary texts, not instead of them.

David T. Runia
1986
Philo of Alexandria and the Timaeus of Plato

"The definitive study of Philo's Platonic background. Runia traces, passage by passage, how Philo reads the Timaeus — Plato's cosmological dialogue — through the lens of Genesis, and Genesis through the lens of the Timaeus. Essential for understanding why Philo's synthesis was philosophically serious rather than superficially eclectic. Dense but rewarding. If you read one secondary work on Philo, this is the one that shows you the engine."

David T. Runia
1993
Philo in Early Christian Literature

Traces Philo's influence on early Christian theology — how his Logos vocabulary, his allegorical method, and his philosophical framework were absorbed by Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Eusebius, Ambrose, and others. The transmission chain from Philo to the Christian intellectual tradition is documented here with scholarly precision. Read this after you have a working knowledge of the primary texts.

"Adam Kamesar, ed."
2009
The Cambridge Companion to Philo

"Multi-author introduction covering Philo's philosophical, exegetical, historical, and political dimensions. Good for breadth — each chapter is by a different specialist, so you get multiple angles rather than a single interpretive line. Useful as a reference after encountering Philo in the primary texts. Not a substitute for reading him."

C.H. Dodd
1953
The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel

"The classic work connecting Philo's Logos to John's Prologue. Part I maps the background — Hellenistic Judaism, the Hermetic literature, Philo, Rabbinic Judaism — that shaped the Fourth Gospel's conceptual world. Dodd is careful, learned, and readable. Published in 1953 and still cited as the starting point for the Philo-John question. Essential if the relationship between the two is your primary interest."

Suggested reading path

A defensible working knowledge of Philo requires less raw volume than Origen — his texts are more concentrated, and the Logos theology is present nearly everywhere. The sequence below is ordered for conceptual development. Four to six weeks of serious reading at a sustainable pace.

01
On the Creation of the World

"Start here. The Logos theology at its clearest: the intelligible world as the content of the divine Reason, the architect analogy, the \"idea of ideas.\" This is Philo's Genesis 1 commentary and the foundation of everything else he builds. Read slowly through sections IV-VI and XXIII."

02
Who Is the Heir of Divine Things

The Logos as divider and mediator. Sections 205-231 are the essential core — the razor-sharp Word that carves reality into form, and the mediator standing on the boundary between God and creation. This is where Philo's Logos becomes most architecturally precise.

03
"Dodd, The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel — Part I"

Read the secondary guide here, between the primary texts. Dodd maps the intellectual background of John's Prologue — Philo, the Wisdom tradition, Hellenistic Judaism. This contextualizes what you've read and sets up the comparison with John. Part I only; the full book can come later.

04
Allegorical Interpretation I-III

"The method in action. Philo's allegorical reading of Genesis 2-3 shows how he reads Torah through philosophical categories. The content matters — the chain of participation from God through Logos to human mind — but so does the method, which passes to Clement and Origen and through them to the whole tradition."

05
On Flight and Finding

"The Logos as high priest and \"eldest Son.\" The most extended cosmological treatment — and the passage (section 109) that explicitly connects logos and sophia, holding both names together for the one mediating reality. Read this after the others; it synthesizes themes already encountered."

06
On the Embassy to Gaius

Read last. Philo the man, in history — leading the Jewish embassy to Caligula, facing imperial madness with composure. No Logos theology here, but it completes the portrait. The philosopher was also a political leader who put his life at risk. The thinking and the living were not separate operations.

Synthesis & closing questions

Philo matters for this project not as a historical curiosity but as the man who built the bridge. Without his synthesis, the Logos tradition has a gap in the middle — you can see the Stoic origins and you can see the Johannine destination, but the mechanism of transfer is invisible. Philo makes it visible. He shows that "In the beginning was the Logos" is not a Christian invention but a philosophical claim with deep roots in both Greek thought and Hebrew scripture, brought into explicit alignment by a Jewish thinker decades before the Gospel of John existed.

The questions below are not review questions. They pull threads that run from Philo into the larger inquiry.

questions to carry forward
On the hinge
Philo demonstrated that the Torah and Greek philosophy point at the same rational structure. The author of John used that demonstration as the platform for the incarnation claim. Does the incarnation complete Philo's system or break it? Is John 1:14 an extension of Philo's logic or a violation of it? Both answers have consequences.
On transcendence
"Philo insists that God is utterly beyond predication — beyond qualities, beyond existence itself as a category. The Logos is how the unknowable becomes knowable. On the consciousness-first commitment, is this the same distinction as Ground (Level 0) and Logos-as-render-order (Level 1)? If so, Philo was mapping the ontological architecture seventeen centuries before the vocabulary existed to name it in those terms."
On the forms
"Philo relocates the Platonic Forms into the Logos — they are not independent eternal objects but the content of the divine mind. If consciousness is the ground and physical reality is the render, then Philo's \"intelligible world within the Logos\" is a first-century description of the render's source code: the rational structure that precedes and generates the physical world. Is this convergence, or is it projection?"
On personhood
"Philo's Logos hovers between the personal and the impersonal — it is \"firstborn Son\" and \"high priest\" but also \"the idea of ideas\" and \"God's shadow.\" The Christian tradition will eventually decide: the Logos is personal. Kastrup's analytic idealism will call universal consciousness \"mind at large\" — impersonal but not mindless. Where does the personal language earn its place, and where is it a convenience?"
On the tradition
Philo was preserved by Christians who found his Logos theology useful. His own tradition — Rabbinic Judaism — largely set him aside. The irony is structural: the man who synthesized Torah and philosophy was preserved by the tradition that used his synthesis for its own purposes, and forgotten by the tradition he was trying to serve. What does it mean that the Logos tradition's most important architect was himself a casualty of the tradition's success?

The thread that runs through all of it: Philo bet his intellectual life on the claim that the God of Abraham and the Logos of the philosophers are not two things but one — that the Torah and Greek philosophy are independent witnesses to a single rational structure underlying reality. The author of John inherited that bet and raised it: the rational structure took on flesh. Whether that escalation completes or contradicts Philo's framework is one of the oldest open questions in the Logos tradition.

It is still open. Philo left enough on the page to keep working with.
