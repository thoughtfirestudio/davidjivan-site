The Shroud of Turin — the medieval painting with a paper trail

David Jivan · davidjivan.net

The Shroud of Turin might be the most studied object in Christian history. Billions of dollars of science, decades of forensic work, a dedicated research field, and for millions of people it settles a question: this is what Jesus looked like. A photograph taken by God at the moment of the resurrection.

There's a problem with that picture. The shroud has a paper trail — and the paper trail starts with the people who made it.

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

What it is

First, what's actually there. The shroud is a linen cloth about fourteen feet long and three and a half feet wide. On it is the faint image of a man — front and back, head to head, the two views laid out so the crown of the head in each image meets in the middle. The hands are crossed over the pelvis. The wrists and fingers are clearly visible.

The image itself is a straw-yellow discoloration of the topmost fibers of the linen — not paint you can see, not a stain that soaked through. It looks like a scorch mark, but it isn't one. It's remarkably shallow: the discoloration touches only the highest fibers of the threads and doesn't penetrate more than a fraction of the depth of a single fiber.

And it's a photographic negative. When the amateur photographer Secondo Pia first photographed it in 1898, the negative of his photograph showed a clear, positive, lifelike face. The image on the cloth is light-reversed: the darkest marks on the cloth are where the body was closest to it. This is the property that made people think of miracles, and it's worth taking seriously, because it's real. The question is whether it takes a miracle to produce it.

When it appeared

Here's the part of the story that doesn't get told in the documentaries. The shroud has no documented history before the 1350s. Nothing. No mention in any surviving text, no inventory, no reference in any church record for the thirteen centuries between the first century and the fourteenth.

It surfaces in Lirey, a village in Champagne in northern France, around 1353 to 1355. It was exhibited in a collegiate church endowed by a knight named Geoffroi de Charny I, who died at the Battle of Poitiers in 1356. The dean of the church, Robert de Caillac, was showing the cloth to pilgrims — and taking their offerings.

Relics were big business in the Middle Ages. The Fourth Lateran Council condemned counterfeit relics in 1215, which tells you how common they were. A 14th-century church with a relic on display had a revenue stream: pilgrims came, they gave, the church got richer. Patrick Geary's classic study of the relic economy, Furta Sacra, documents how relics were bought, sold, stolen, and counterfeited along the trade routes. This was a known industry with known techniques. And the church in Lirey had just acquired the most valuable relic imaginable: the burial cloth of Jesus Christ.

What was said about it at the time

This is where the paper trail starts. Within a few years of the cloth appearing at Lirey, the bishop of Troyes — the diocese Lirey sat in — investigated. His name was Henri de Poitiers. He looked into where the cloth came from and who made it, and he concluded it was a fake. He suppressed the exhibition.

Decades later, in 1389, his successor Pierre d'Arcis wrote a formal memorandum to the pope. It's a surviving document — you can read the full text today. D'Arcis reports that Henri de Poitiers had "discovered the fraud and how the said cloth had been cunningly painted, the truth being attested by the artist who had painted it, to wit, that it was a work of human skill and not miraculously wrought or bestowed."

Read that again. The bishop of Troyes investigated the shroud. He found the artist. The artist confessed to painting it. That's not a skeptic's inference. That's the report of a bishop to the pope, citing his predecessor's investigation.

The pope at the time was Clement VII in Avignon. He didn't ban the cloth — and that's often spun as a victory for the shroud. But read his ruling. In January 1390 he allowed the cloth to be shown only on one condition: that the priests announce to the crowd that it was not the true burial cloth of the Lord, but "a figure or representation" of the shroud. The papacy officially downgraded the object to a painting. It could be displayed as a devotional image. It could not be presented as the real thing.

And in 2025, a historian named Nicolas Sarzeaud published something remarkable: a newly discovered passage by Nicole Oresme, one of the most prominent philosophers of the 14th century, who wrote in the 1370s — before d'Arcis — that the shroud was a "clear" and "patent" fake, and that the clergy behind it were deceiving people "in order to elicit offerings for their churches." This is now the earliest known written rejection of the shroud, and it's independent of the whole bishop-vs-church dispute.

The documentary record, in one line: the shroud appears in the 1350s, its local bishop investigated it and found the artist, who confessed; his successor reported the confession to the pope; the pope ruled it could only be shown as a representation; and the sharpest philosopher of the age called it a patent fake. All within its own lifetime.

What the science says

The scientific case runs in the same direction, and it starts with the thing people forget to mention: in 1988, the Vatican itself allowed the cloth to be carbon-dated.

Three independent accelerator mass spectrometry laboratories — Oxford, Arizona, and Zurich — tested a sample of the linen, coordinated by the British Museum. Their results were published in Nature in 1989. All three labs agreed: the linen dates to between 1260 and 1390 CE, with 95% confidence. The paper's conclusion is blunt: "conclusive evidence that the linen of the Shroud of Turin is mediaeval."

That range matches the cloth's appearance at Lirey in the 1350s almost perfectly. The flax was grown, spun, and woven in the 13th or 14th century. If the shroud wrapped a first-century body, the linen should date to the first century. It doesn't.

Every challenge to the dating has been examined. A claim that the sample was cut from a medieval repair patch — tested against the actual retained samples by Freer-Waters and Jull in 2010, found to be authentic original textile. Contamination theories, bacterial films, carbon monoxide — all tested, all rejected. A 2022 X-ray study claiming a first-century date comes from a group whose earlier paper was retracted, and it's not taken seriously by dating experts. The physicist who ran the Oxford lab put it plainly: none of the challenges to the 1988 result "stack up."

And here's the part the shroud's defenders lean on hardest, the 3D data and the photographic negative — both turn out to be exactly what you'd expect from a contact transfer. The VP-8 image analyzer famously reads the shroud's image as three-dimensional, because darker marks correspond to closer parts of the body. But that's not a miracle; that's physics. If you press cloth against a three-dimensional surface — a bas-relief, a statue, a body — the cloth touches the highest points hardest and transfers more material there. Darkness tracks distance. Any rubbing of a 3D object should look 3D in the analyzer. In 2009, the Italian chemist Luigi Garlaschelli did exactly this with medieval materials: acidic pigment rubbed onto linen draped over a volunteer and a bas-relief face, aged in an oven, washed. The result was a faint, superficial, non-pigmented image with photographic negativity, 3D data, and no visible pigment — the same properties as the original. Joe Nickell had demonstrated the same technique with bas-relief rubbings back in the 1970s.

The blood is the weakest pillar of the whole authenticity case. STURP, the 1978 research team, famously announced the blood was real — but the team's own microscopist, Walter McCrone, found it was pigment: red ochre and vermilion, the standard medieval painting materials. As of 2025, it has not been scientifically demonstrated that the "blood" is human blood at all. And in 2019, a bloodstain-pattern analysis by Matteo Borrini and Luigi Garlaschelli — the same Garlaschelli who reproduced the shroud — concluded the rivulets "cannot be real bloodstains" and look handmade.

The science and the documents agree. The carbon dating says the linen is medieval. The image chemistry says it's a contact transfer. The 14th-century records say the artist confessed.

What this points to

So here's where I land, and I want to be careful about it, because I know this feels like it should be a bigger deal than it is.

The shroud is a medieval artifact. The evidence for that is overwhelming, and it was known — in writing, by the church's own bishops — within decades of the thing appearing. It's not a mysterious object that science hasn't explained. It's an explained object: a contact-transfer image on 14th-century linen, made by an artist who confessed, for a church that needed a relic and had a template to copy.

What interests me is what the shroud shows us about medieval Christianity — which is the same thing that interests me about the whole Christian story. Relics were how medieval faith worked. Not a corruption of it, a feature of it. The acheiropoieta tradition — the Mandylion, the Veil of Veronica, "images made without hands" — had been running for eight centuries by the time Lirey got its shroud. The forger had a genre to work in, an audience expecting the genre, and a church willing to display the result. The relic economy was the medieval equivalent of a subscription business: a steady stream of pilgrims, a steady stream of offerings.

And here's the thing I keep coming back to. The people who ran that church were not stupid. The bishop investigated and found the artist. The pope's court read the report. The sharpest philosopher in Europe saw through it. The church knew — and showed it anyway. That's not a scandal to me. That's the shape of institutional religion in every century, including this one: the institution knows more than it can afford to say, and what it can't say, it displays.

Does any of this touch the actual claims of Christianity? I don't think it does. The shroud was never a pillar of Christian faith — the tradition doesn't hang on it, the creeds don't mention it, and the church itself ruled in 1390 that it was a painting. Whether Jesus rose from the dead is a question I hold openly, and the shroud doesn't move it one inch. It's a 14th-century object with a 14th-century story. It tells us how medieval Christianity worked. It tells us nothing about what happened in the first century.

What it does tell us — and this is the part I actually value — is that honesty about religious objects doesn't destroy faith. The people who venerated the shroud in 1390 were told, by the pope's own ruling, that it was a representation, and they kept venerating it. Faith survived the truth. It usually does. The thing that kills faith is the lie — the claim that the object is the real thing when the paper trail says otherwise.

The shroud is a painting. The records say so. The science says so. The interesting question was never whether it's real. It's what it shows us about a church that knew, and showed it anyway.
