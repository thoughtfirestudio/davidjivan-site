The argument, at a glance

One claim, three threads

Read it like an essay: the claim first, then the evidence, point by point.

The claim

No part of John describes Jesus as the very God of Israel. The reading that fits every passage is a hybrid Logos/image Christology — Jesus as the divine Logos and the divine image.

A · The Logos

A distinct, subordinate being who can bear the title theos — John 1:1's grammar, read the same way in the tradition that runs from Philo before him to Justin, Origen, and Eusebius after.

B · The Image

To see him is to see the Father — oneness as mutual indwelling, and the image shared: you become a child of God.

C · The Name

The angel who bears the divine name — Thomas's confession, and the "I am" that is a code, not a claim.

D · What it means

The Trinity is a later answer to a later question. John's own gospel: image, indwelling, divinization.

The honest wall

Divine identity (Hurtado, Bauckham) and Romans 9:5 — the strongest counter-evidence, displayed fairly.

The whole thing, in plain words

What this is actually saying

John's Jesus is not God-the-Father wearing a costume. He's the image of God — the one who carries God's presence so completely that to see him is to see the Father, and who says the same oneness is open to anyone who follows him. You become a child of God. That's the gospel John is actually laying down.

The Trinity — one being, three persons — is a later answer to a later question, born in the second century out of Greek philosophy. It may be true. But it's not what John wrote. And knowing the difference isn't losing anything. It's finally hearing the gospel's own voice.

The Greek room

Learn the grammar behind the walk

You don't need to read Greek to hear what John is saying — you need about twenty words and three ideas. Six short lessons, no homework. Click a lesson to open it.

01The alphabet — sound it out

Greek has 24 letters. This exhibit uses about fifteen of them. Learn these and you can sound out every Greek word in the museum:

Now read the exhibit's vocabulary aloud: theos (God), logos (word), kyrios (lord), ēn (was), kai (and), sarx (flesh), aiōn (age). That's the whole museum in seven words.

02The article — ho, hē, to, and the three jobs

ho / hē / to = “the” (masculine / feminine / neuter). Same idea as English — but Greek nouns also change their endings to show their job in the sentence. You need three jobs:

nominativeho theos“the God” — the subject
genitivetou theou“of the God” — possession
accusativeton theon“the God” — the object

That's why “the God” keeps changing shape: ho theos, tou theou, ton theon — same word, three jobs. In John 1:1 the Word is “with ton theon” — accusative, article present. The article declines. Its absence is the signal.

03Why Greek drops the article

Here's the correction that matters: anarthrous — no article — does not automatically mean “not God.” Greek drops articles constantly. Paul writes “from theos our Father” (Rom 1:7) with no article, and it's unambiguously the true God.

So the rule “article = the God, no article = a god” is too flat. The real signals are the construction (where the word sits), the context, and the contrast inside the sentence. That's the next lesson.

Counter-example to remember: Rom 1:7 — “ἀπὸ θεοῦ πατρὸς ἡμῶν” — “from God our Father,” anarthrous, meaning the God of Israel.

04The predicate — the John 1:1 machinery

Word order matters. In “kai theos ēn ho logos,” the word theos comes before the verb ēn (“was”) and refers back to “the Word” (ho logos, article present).

When a predicate word precedes the verb, Greek often drops its article even when the thing is definite — and grammarians (Colwell, then Harner) showed the result reads as qualitative: it describes the kind, not the identity. The whole sentence, three clauses:

Fairness note: this grammar isn't the contested part. Daniel Wallace — the best-known defender of “the Word was God” — agrees the anarthrous predicate nominative is qualitative. He reads it as the Word sharing the divine essence, a distinct person. The real disagreement is what “qualitative” meant to a first-century Jew — see Point A, A7.

1 · en archē ēn ho logos — "in the beginning was the Word"
2 · kai ho logos ēn pros ton theon — "the Word was with the God" — article present; inside the preposition, after the verb
3 · kai theos ēn ho logos — "and divine was the Word" — no article; before the verb: the predicate

Only clause 3 puts theos before the verb. Clause 2's ton theon is a prepositional object — arthrous, definite, the God the Word is with. The contrast between the two clauses is the whole point.

Read it aloud and the structure does the work: ho logos keeps its article in both clauses where it appears — ho logos... ho logos. And theos? Bare, once, in the middle. The ear catches what the eye can miss. If John had meant “the Word was the God,” the rhythm would have demanded the article — and it isn't there.

Compare the two lines John could have written:

kai theos ēn ho logos — “and divine was the Word” — what John wrote
kai ho theos ēn ho logos — “and THE God was the Word” — what John didn't write

That's the whole exhibit in two lines: same words, one little article, two completely different claims.

05Doxologies — the Romans 9:5 payoff

When the New Testament blesses God — “Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ” — it normally uses the article: ho theos eulogētos (1 Pet 1:3; 2 Cor 1:3; Rom 1:25).

Paul's line in Romans 9:5 is the odd one out: theos eulogētos, anarthrous. Which cuts two ways — it's why some read it as a blessing of the Father, and it's why most read it as a predication of Christ: “who is God over all, blessed.” Same missing article, different construction, different outcome. The grammar is a pattern, not a machine.

The full debate is on the counterweight wall → Romans 9:5.

07The parallel — John 10:33

The Jews' accusation against Jesus uses the exact grammar the whole exhibit is about — twice, in parallel:

σὺ ἄνθρωπος ὢν ποιεῖς σεαυτὸν θεόν
sy anthrōpos ōn poieis seauton theon
you · a human · being · you make · yourself · a god

Anthrōpos — “a human” — no article. Theon — “a god” — no article. The charge itself says: “You, being a human, make yourself a god.” If they had wanted to accuse him of claiming to be the God, they had the word for it — ton theon. They didn't use it.

So even the accusation runs on the same anarthrous grammar as John 1:1 — which is exactly why Jesus answers with Psalm 82: “your own law says humans can be gods.” See Point B, B2.

The museum's other voice

Scholarship disagrees with itself here.

The reading you've just walked — divine agency, a subordinate Logos who bears the title — faces a strong rival school: divine identity. Larry Hurtado (Lord Jesus Christ) and Richard Bauckham (Jesus and the God of Israel) argue that first-century believers included Jesus within the identity of the one God — not as a second being beside him.

Both can't be right. The exhibit shows you the evidence; the argument is still open.

The question everyone asks

What about Romans 9:5?

Paul writes of Christ: “who is over all, God blessed forever” — ho ōn epi pantōn Theos eulogētos. Isn't that Paul calling Jesus God?

Two readings, split by a comma that isn't in the original:

Christological (the majority): “the Christ... who is over all, God blessed” — Christ called theos. Doxological: “the Christ according to the flesh — God who is over all be blessed” — a blessing of the Father, the way Paul blesses God elsewhere.

The twist: blessing-formulas in the New Testament are normally arthrous — 1 Pet 1:3: “Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ.” Paul's anarthrous theos eulogētos is the odd one — a real argument that it's a predication of Christ, not a blessing of the Father. The same missing article, working differently in a different construction.

Either way, it's Paul — a different author, a different document. The museum's claim is scoped to the Gospel of John. And even at its highest, Romans 9:5 ascribes divine status within first-century Jewish monotheism; the machinery of one-ousia Trinitarianism is still two centuries away. It's the divine-identity school's strongest text — and it belongs here, on the wall of honest disagreement.
